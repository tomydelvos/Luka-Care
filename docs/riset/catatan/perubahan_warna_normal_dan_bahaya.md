## 1. Normal progression for minor cuts, abrasions and blisters (Day 0–3, Day 3–7, Weeks 1–3, Weeks–months)

### Takeaway
Official sources give phase-level timing rather than precise day-by-day colours: inflammation runs from injury to about day 5, the proliferation phase (red granular base, edges pulling in, new skin covering the surface) runs to about 3 weeks, and scar remodelling lasts months, with the scar going from red and thick to pale and thin. For brown skin, the main long-term colour change is post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH). Brown PIH can take months to years to fade, and deeper blue-grey PIH can be permanent. No authoritative public-facing source gave a specific "normal" width for the redness rim.

### Cited Findings
**Overall timing (phases)**
- The Indonesian Ministry of Health (Ditjen Kesehatan Lanjutan) article "Jenis dan Fase Penyembuhan Luka" (21 Dec 2022, nurse author, RSJ Dr. Radjiman Wediodiningrat Lawang) gives three phases. (1) **Inflammation**, from injury to about **day 5**: haemostasis (vessels constrict, platelets clump, a fibrin mesh and clot form) followed by inflammation (vessels dilate and leak more, neutrophils and macrophages clear bacteria and debris). (2) **Proliferation/fibroplasia**, from the end of inflammation to about **3 weeks**: new capillaries form, **red granulation tissue** forms with fibroblasts making collagen, wound edges contract inward, and new epithelium covers the surface (re-epithelialisation). (3) **Maturation/remodelling**, from the end of proliferation and **lasting months**: excess collagen and capillaries are cleared and contraction reaches its maximum. — [Kemenkes Keslan, Jenis dan Fase Penyembuhan Luka (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
- During maturation the scar changes from **red and thick to pale and thin**. A healed scar reaches only about **80%** of normal skin's tensile strength ("Jaringan parut pada luka yang sembuh tidak akan mencapai kekuatan regang kulit normal, tetapi hanya mencapai 80%"). — [Kemenkes Keslan (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
- The same article classes abrasions, lacerations and uncomplicated surgical wounds as **acute wounds** that usually heal well without complications. Bruises (contusions) and haematomas are classed as **closed wounds**. — [Kemenkes Keslan (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)

**Public-facing timing for cuts and grazes**
- NHS: "Most cuts and grazes can be treated at home and will start to heal in a few days." A dressing can come off "after a few days, once the wound has closed." — [NHS, Cuts and grazes (reviewed 2 Apr 2026)](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cuts-and-grazes/)

**Normal discharge (what is not alarming)**
- A nursing wound-assessment grading (Morison 2003, reproduced by Kemenkes) rates exudate as follows. **Minimal** is none, or **non-purulent** fluid covering up to a quarter of the dressing. **Moderate** is **yellowish exudate** ("eksudat berwarna kekuningan") covering up to half the dressing. **Heavy** is **purulent** exudate covering more than half the dressing. — [Kemenkes Keslan, Tanda-tanda Infeksi pada Luka (21 Dec 2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1970/tanda-tanda-infeksi-pada-luka)
- In IWII terms, wound **contamination or colonisation** shows "No delay in wound healing", meaning bacteria on a wound without a delay in healing are not infection. — [NHS Forth Valley Wound Infection Pathway, citing IWII 2022 (uploaded Feb 2026)](https://nhsforthvalley.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/NHS-Forth-Valley-Wound-Infection-Pathway.pdf); in contamination "There are no clinical signs and symptoms as the healing trajectory continues as expected" — [Haesler & Ousey, Wounds International 2018 (IWII 2016 continuum)](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)

**How much redness is normal (no consensus number found)**
- The Kemenkes/Morison grading rates redness limited to the immediate wound surroundings and **not more than 0.5 cm** from the wound as "moderate". Redness that spreads **more than 0.5 cm** ("meluas lebih dari 0,5 cm dari luka") is rated "severe". — [Kemenkes Keslan (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1970/tanda-tanda-infeksi-pada-luka)
- IWII 2022 (summarised in "Made Easy") defines **local** infection as confined to the wound and periwound "less than 2cm". **Spreading** infection has "spread greater than 2cm from a wound". — [Ousey, Swanson & Sussman, Wound Infection in Clinical Practice Made Easy, Wounds International (Nov 2022)](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2023/02/ebdcfc5426783d6a07e91c29d174bf89.pdf)

**Later colour change on brown skin: post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH)**
- PIH is "an acquired hypermelanosis occurring after cutaneous inflammation or injury". It "more frequently affects skin-of-color patients" (Fitzpatrick IV–VI) and can be more severe in them. — [Davis & Callender, J Clin Aesthet Dermatol 2010;3(7):20–31](https://jcadonline.com/postinflammatory-hyperpigmentation-a-review-of-the-epidemiology-clinical-features-and-treatment-options-in-skin-of-color/)
- **Epidermal PIH** looks "tan, brown, or dark brown" and "may take months to years to resolve without treatment". **Dermal PIH** "has a blue-gray appearance" and "may either be permanent or resolve over a protracted period of time if left untreated." — [Davis & Callender 2010](https://jcadonline.com/postinflammatory-hyperpigmentation-a-review-of-the-epidemiology-clinical-features-and-treatment-options-in-skin-of-color/)
- A Singapore study found PIH **more common in darker-skinned Malay and Indian people than in Chinese people**, which suggests skin pigmentation matters more than ethnicity. In a 2002 acne study, about 47% of Asian patients (n=19) and about 65% of African American patients (n=239) developed PIH. — [Davis & Callender 2010](https://jcadonline.com/postinflammatory-hyperpigmentation-a-review-of-the-epidemiology-clinical-features-and-treatment-options-in-skin-of-color/)
- "PIH can worsen with ultraviolet (UV) irradiation". The review recommends daily broad-spectrum sunscreen of at least SPF 30, sun avoidance and protective clothing. — [Davis & Callender 2010](https://jcadonline.com/postinflammatory-hyperpigmentation-a-review-of-the-epidemiology-clinical-features-and-treatment-options-in-skin-of-color/)

### Inferences
- A practical lay timeline can be assembled from the Kemenkes phase timing. Days 0–5: an inflamed edge with a clot or scab is expected. Days 5–21: a red granular base or pink new skin closes the wound from the edges. After that, months of scar remodelling. This is phase-level timing, not a validated day-by-day colour chart.
- In Kemenkes/Morison, ≤0.5 cm redness is still rated "moderate", which is a severity score, not "normal". In IWII, <2 cm marks *local infection* when other signs are present, not a normal rim. Neither is a "normal rim" definition. A cautious lay message is that a thin rim of redness hugging the edge in the first few days is common, and redness that **grows** after day 2–3, or spreads well beyond the edge, needs attention. The "thin rim" wording is an inference and is not sourced.
- Indonesia's population is largely Malay-origin and medium-to-dark skinned. The Singapore Malay/Indian finding suggests LukaCare readers are at relatively high risk of PIH after wounds, so sun protection of healing skin is a relevant tip.

### Gaps
- No authoritative public source found that gives a numeric "normal" redness width for minor wounds. The AAD, healthdirect and Cleveland Clinic pages that might do so were blocked.
- No sourced day-by-day description was retrieved for abrasion scab formation, **pink new skin**, or **itching** during healing. AAD and healthdirect pages could not be read.
- No sourced timing was retrieved for scar fading in months (e.g., the often-quoted 12–18 months). Kemenkes says only "months". The AAD scar page was blocked.
- No sourced normal progression was found for **blisters** (intact roof, fluid reabsorption, peeling).

## 2. Normal yellow fibrin/slough or serous fluid vs pus; green/cloudy/smelly discharge; spreading redness, red streaks, dark/black/grey skin, blistering; fever

### Takeaway
The IWII continuum, cited by a 2026 NHS pathway, lists warning signs in three tiers. **Local** signs are redness, warmth, swelling, pus, and new or increasing pain or smell. **Spreading** signs are redness or hardening extending beyond about 2 cm, red lymphatic tracking (lymphangitis), swollen glands, crackling under the skin (crepitus), and the wound breaking down. **Systemic** signs are fever, malaise and sepsis. Thin yellowish fluid in small amounts is graded as minimal-to-moderate. Pus (purulent discharge), a pungent smell, or a new smell are infection signs.

### Cited Findings
**IWII infection continuum**
- **Covert (subtle) local infection** signs: hypergranulation (excessive "vascular" tissue), **bleeding friable granulation**, epithelial bridging/pocketing, wound breakdown and enlargement, "delayed wound healing beyond expectations", "new or increasing pain", and "increasing malodour". — [Haesler & Ousey, Wounds International 2018 (IWII 2016)](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)
- **Overt (classic) local infection** signs: erythema, local warmth, swelling, **purulent discharge**, delayed healing, new or increasing pain, and increasing malodour. — [Haesler & Ousey 2018](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf). The IWII 2022-based version lists erythema, warmth to touch, swelling, purulent exudate, and new or increased pain as overt signs, and hypergranulation, bleeding, increased exudate, delayed healing and erythema as covert signs. — [NHS Forth Valley Wound Infection Pathway (IWII 2022)](https://nhsforthvalley.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/NHS-Forth-Valley-Wound-Infection-Pathway.pdf)
- **Spreading infection** signs: "Spreading erythema", wound breakdown, **swollen lymph glands**, hardening of tissue (induration), **lymphangitis**, and **crepitus**. — [NHS Forth Valley (IWII 2022)](https://nhsforthvalley.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/NHS-Forth-Valley-Wound-Infection-Pathway.pdf). IWII 2016 also lists malaise, lethargy, loss of appetite, and wound dehiscence with or without satellite lesions. Lymphangitis is defined as "inflammation of the lymph glands and lymphatic vessel walls". — [Haesler & Ousey 2018](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)
- **Spreading threshold:** infection has "spread greater than 2cm from a wound". — [IWII 2022 Made Easy, Wounds International (2022)](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2023/02/ebdcfc5426783d6a07e91c29d174bf89.pdf)
- **Systemic infection** signs: malaise, lethargy or general deterioration, loss of appetite, **fever/pyrexia**, severe sepsis, septic shock, and organ failure. One sign is enough to warrant urgent medical review. — [NHS Forth Valley (IWII 2022)](https://nhsforthvalley.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/NHS-Forth-Valley-Wound-Infection-Pathway.pdf); "Signs of systemic infection include sepsis", and referral and immediate treatment are "vital". — [IWII 2022 Made Easy](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2023/02/ebdcfc5426783d6a07e91c29d174bf89.pdf)

**Discharge and smell**
- "Purulence and malodour are produced by bacteria processes." — [Haesler & Ousey 2018](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)
- Malodour is expected when dead (devitalised) tissue is present, but **odour in a clean wound, or a new odour, may indicate infection**. — [NHS Forth Valley (IWII 2022)](https://nhsforthvalley.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/NHS-Forth-Valley-Wound-Infection-Pathway.pdf)
- Odour grading (Morison via Kemenkes): none = mild; noticeable only when the dressing is opened = moderate; "bau yang menusuk" (a pungent smell) even with the dressing on = severe. — [Kemenkes Keslan (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1970/tanda-tanda-infeksi-pada-luka)
- In clinical practice, **slough** (yellow devitalised tissue) is handled by debridement and is not itself listed as an infection sign. **Purulent exudate** is listed as an overt sign of infection. — [NHS Forth Valley (IWII 2022)](https://nhsforthvalley.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/NHS-Forth-Valley-Wound-Infection-Pathway.pdf)
- "Friable granulation tissue can occur due to bacterial burden interfering with healthy collagen formation", so a wound bed that bleeds easily can be an early sign of infection. — [Haesler & Ousey 2018](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)

**Lay-facing warning signs**
- NHS: get an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111 if "a cut is swollen, red and getting more painful or pus is coming out of it". Also if "a wound has soil, pus or body fluids in it, or it's still dirty after cleaning it", or "you've cut yourself and also feel generally unwell or have a high temperature". — [NHS, Cuts and grazes (2026)](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cuts-and-grazes/)
- HEE cellulitis leaflet: seek urgent help for "High temperature", "Enlarging or hardening of the reddened area", chills with heavy sweating, increased pain, **numbness when the area is touched**, or infection that does not improve with antibiotics. — [Health Education England, Cellulitis leaflet (undated)](https://www.hee.nhs.uk/sites/default/files/documents/Cellulitis.pdf)
- Pain that is always present ("nyeri selalu dirasakan pasien") or that spreads beyond the wound is graded as severe. Prominent swelling with redness is also graded as severe. — [Kemenkes Keslan (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1970/tanda-tanda-infeksi-pada-luka)

**Dark purple, black or grey skin; blistering**
- NICE: refer urgently to hospital for signs suggesting more serious illness, including **necrotising fasciitis** or **sepsis**. — [NICE NG141, Cellulitis and erysipelas: antimicrobial prescribing, recs 1.1.13](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng141/chapter/Recommendations)
- In darker skin, "extra care should be taken to ensure that eschar or necrosis are not incorrectly diagnosed or missed altogether". — [Dhoonmoon & Fletcher, Wounds International 2022;13(2)](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/862f76fc165d6b13996911491dc55846.pdf)
- In skin of colour, look for **blisters, bullae, or "punched out" lesions with violaceous margins**. — [Sangha, J Clin Aesthet Dermatol 2025;18(5–6 Suppl 1):28–29](https://jcadonline.com/diagnosing-skin-infections-in-skin-of-color/)

### Inferences
- The sources support a three-tier lay message. (a) A **wound that is getting worse instead of better** (more pain, more swelling, more fluid, new smell, pus) needs a same-day check. (b) **Redness or hardness that keeps spreading** (beyond about 2 cm, or growing past a pen line), red lines, or swollen glands also need a same-day or urgent check. (c) **Fever, chills, feeling very unwell**, or skin turning dark purple, black or grey, blistering, or going numb means emergency care.
- "Lymphangitis" is the clinical term for what lay readers would see as a red (on brown skin, possibly darker) **line or streak tracking from the wound toward the armpit or groin**. The "streak" description is a standard plain-language gloss but was not quoted from a fetched source.
- Small amounts of thin clear or pale-yellow fluid fit Morison's "minimal/moderate" grades. Thick, opaque (cloudy) or copious discharge fits "purulent". The green-discharge point is not sourced (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No fetched source specifically addressed **green** discharge (often attributed to *Pseudomonas*). This should not be stated without a source.
- No fetched source gave a plain-language distinction between **fibrin** (normal yellow film on a healing bed) and **pus** for lay readers.
- The CDC necrotizing fasciitis page (early signs: rapidly spreading red/warm swollen area, pain out of proportion; later: blisters, black spots, skin colour change) and the CDC/NHS sepsis pages were **blocked**, so their exact wording and skin-colour descriptors (e.g., NHS: blue/grey/blotchy skin, checking palms/soles/lips on brown skin) could not be verified.
- No source retrieved on **how fast** spreading redness becomes dangerous (e.g., hours vs days).

## 3. Bruise/haematoma colour changes and timing (to avoid confusion with wound colours)

### Takeaway
Bruises usually move from red or purple (fresh) through blue to green, yellow and brown (older). Yellow does not appear before about 18–24 hours. A Thai study (relevant to Southeast Asian skin) found mean times of about 1 day for red, 2 days for purple, 2.5 days for green and 3.5 days for yellow. Clinicians stress that bruise colour cannot reliably date an injury, and skin colour affects how bruises look.

### Cited Findings
- Red and purple suggest a fresher bruise. Bruises then progress through blue, then brown, yellow or green. "Some studies do indicate that yellow will not appear in a bruise until at least 18–24 hours after an injury." Emergency physicians "cannot and should not definitively age a bruise". Appearance varies with tissue type, depth, force, **skin colour**, age, health and medications (anticoagulants, antiplatelets, steroids). — [Riviello, ACEP Now (6 Feb 2014)](https://acepnow.com/article/can-tell-old-bruise-based-color)
- A cross-sectional study of **400 contusions in 199 healthy Thai patients** with known injury time found mean time since injury of **red ~24 h, purple ~48 h, green ~60 h, yellow ~84 h**. Yellow was first seen at a **minimum of 18 hours**. Colour correlated significantly with time (p<0.001), and colours were assessed by naked eye. — [Dulabutr, Thammasat Medical Journal 2018;18(4):561–570](https://he02.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/tmj/article/view/162287)
- Kemenkes classes contusions (memar) and haematomas as closed wounds, with skin intact and tissue beneath damaged. Haematoma is blood collecting outside blood vessels. — [Kemenkes Keslan, Jenis dan Fase Penyembuhan Luka (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka); [Kemenkes Keslan, Tanda-tanda Infeksi pada Luka (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1970/tanda-tanda-infeksi-pada-luka)

### Inferences
- The green and yellow of a resolving bruise around a wound are expected and are not "infection colours", as long as the area is getting less painful and less swollen. A bruise that grows, becomes harder, hotter or more painful behaves like the infection signs in Section 2.
- On brown skin, fresh bruises may look darker (deep purple, brownish or black) rather than red. The ACEP piece names skin colour as a factor, but no source retrieved describes bruise colours on brown skin specifically.

### Gaps
- No fetched source gave **total resolution time** for a typical bruise (commonly quoted as about 2 weeks). The Cleveland Clinic and NHS bruise pages were blocked.
- No fetched source described the **haemoglobin → biliverdin (green) → bilirubin (yellow) → haemosiderin (brown)** pathway. This is standard physiology but should be sourced before use.
- The original Langlois & Gresham (1991) study (PubMed 1748358), likely the source of the "yellow not before 18 hours" finding, could not be read (reCAPTCHA).

## 4. How redness and inflammation look on darker/brown skin

### Takeaway
On brown and black skin, redness may be faint or invisible. Inflammation may instead look **violaceous (purple), red-brown, dark brown (hyperpigmented) or grey**. Clinicians are told to rely on **touch (warmth, firmness), swelling (tight/shiny skin, changed pore pattern), pain or tenderness, and comparison with the same area on the other side**. NICE explicitly warns that "redness may be less visible on darker skin tones". There is measurable under-detection: infection detection from clinical signs alone was much lower in darker skin (2.9%) than in lighter skin (14.0%) in one wound study.

### Cited Findings
- Erythema "can also present as **violaceous, hyperpigmented (ie, brown-dark brown), or gray**". Assessors should "compare the affected skin to unaffected skin". They should check **warmth** and swelling-related contour change (does the skin look **taut or shiny**?) and touch the skin ("it's important to not only look but touch the skin"). They should look at whether **pores look more prominent or more widely spaced** (subtle oedema), ask about pain, tenderness and itch, and ask about fever, nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea. The absence of one or two cues should not lower suspicion. — [Sangha, J Clin Aesthet Dermatol 2025](https://jcadonline.com/diagnosing-skin-infections-in-skin-of-color/)
- Johnson et al. (2024; 350 chronic wounds), as reported by Sangha: "erythema was reported less frequently with increased [Fitzpatrick] score". Sensitivity of clinical signs alone for detecting infection was **2.9% in high-Fitzpatrick vs 14.0% in low-Fitzpatrick** patients. — [Sangha 2025](https://jcadonline.com/diagnosing-skin-infections-in-skin-of-color/)
- A 2022 survey of over 200 wound-care clinicians found that more than 60% were unaware of skin-tone-specific wound education, and nearly 90% of existing teaching materials showed Fitzpatrick I–II (light) skin. — [Sangha 2025](https://jcadonline.com/diagnosing-skin-infections-in-skin-of-color/)
- When erythema is masked by pigment, look for **red-brown, violaceous, or grayish hues** and compare with normal skin. — [Benjamin & Heath, MDedge/AAP 2020 (23 Oct 2020)](https://mdedge.com/content/how-assess-erythema-children-skin-color)
- International skin-tone survey (Dhoonmoon & Fletcher) findings:
  - Conditions "may not present as red in all skin tones" (p. 7), but "some dark skin tones do show areas of redness" (p. 10), so redness should not be dismissed.
  - "Infection must be identified early by investigating signs and symptoms that do not rely on 'redness'" (p. 9).
  - Their assessment checklist asks: Does the skin feel warm or cool? Is there swelling? Is it spongy or firm? Is there pain, itch or change in sensation? How does the periwound compare to the surrounding skin? It also advises comparing the same anatomical site on the other side and notes that "The senses – especially touch – should also be considered" (p. 7–8).
  - Pallor and "looking pale" are also unreliable on dark skin.
  - [Dhoonmoon & Fletcher, Wounds International 2022;13(2)](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/862f76fc165d6b13996911491dc55846.pdf)
- NICE: "Be aware that redness may be less visible on darker skin tones." — [NICE NG141, rec 1.1.3](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng141/chapter/Recommendations)
- *Mind the Gap: A handbook of clinical signs in Black and Brown skin* (Malone Mukwende, Margot Turner, Peter Tamony; St George's, University of London) was announced 18 June 2020. It teaches that some signs look different on darker skin and that descriptors such as "pale" or "lips turned blue" are not useful for Black patients. — [SGUL news (18 Jun 2020)](https://sgul.ac.uk/news/mind-the-gap-a-handbook-of-clinical-signs-on-black-and-brown-skin); [Mukwende, Wounds UK 16(3), 27 Sep 2020](https://wounds-uk.com/journal-articles/mind-gap-clinical-handbook-signs-and-symptoms-black-and-brown-skin/); handbook hosted at [NHS Learning Hub](https://learninghub.nhs.uk/Resource/2017)

### Inferences
- For LukaCare readers with brown skin, the most reliable home checks are as follows. (1) **Feel**: is the skin around the wound hotter than the same spot on the other limb? (2) **Swelling/tightness/shine** and **firmness** (hard, not soft). (3) **Pain** that is increasing rather than decreasing. (4) **Colour change relative to the person's own normal skin**: darker, purplish, reddish-brown or greyish. (5) **Spread over time**, tracked with a pen line and photos in the same light.
- Natural daylight and side-by-side comparison help, but no retrieved source gives a lighting protocol (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- The full content of *Mind the Gap* (e.g., its specific cellulitis and inflammation descriptions) could not be retrieved, and the NHS pages that now include "on brown and black skin" descriptions (cellulitis, sepsis) were blocked. The report-writer should not quote their exact wording without verification.
- No retrieved source gave a formal **lighting** recommendation for lay assessment.
- No Indonesia-specific study was found on how inflammation looks on Indonesian skin tones.

## 5. When to seek care and how fast; marking the redness edge with a pen; when to suspect tetanus

### Takeaway
**Emergency (999/A&E-equivalent, IGD in Indonesia):** bleeding that won't stop, spurting bright-red blood, numbness or inability to move near the wound, deep cuts on the face or palm, an object stuck in the wound, and signs of sepsis or necrotising infection. **Same-day/urgent:** a wound that is swollen, red and more painful, has pus, is still dirty, is larger than about 5 cm, or comes with feeling unwell or a high temperature. Drawing around the redness with a pen and writing the date is recommended (NICE, HEE) to see whether it spreads. Seek help if it worsens rapidly at any time, or does not start improving within 2–3 days of treatment. Tetanus vaccination is needed for dirty or major wounds if the last dose was 5 or more years ago, and for any wound if 10 or more years ago or vaccination status is unknown.

### Cited Findings
**Urgency tiers (NHS, cuts and grazes)**
- **Call 999 or go to A&E** if:
  - "you have a cut and cannot stop the bleeding"
  - "the blood comes out in spurts and is bright red and hard to control"
  - "you lose feeling near the wound or have trouble moving it"
  - "you have a bad cut on your face or the palm of your hand"
  - "there's something stuck in the cut, such as a shard of glass"
  - NHS also advises: "Do not drive to A&E."
  - [NHS, Cuts and grazes (reviewed 2 Apr 2026)](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cuts-and-grazes/)
- **Urgent GP or NHS 111** if:
  - "a cut is swollen, red and getting more painful or pus is coming out of it"
  - the wound has soil, pus or body fluids in it, or is still dirty after cleaning
  - the person feels generally unwell or has a high temperature
  - the cut is "larger than around 5cm (2 inches)"
  - [NHS, Cuts and grazes (2026)](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cuts-and-grazes/)
- NHS: "You may also need to have an injection to prevent tetanus or be given antibiotics if the cut could be infected." — [NHS, Cuts and grazes (2026)](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cuts-and-grazes/)

**Pen-marking and monitoring**
- NICE: "consider drawing around the extent of the infection with a single-use surgical marker pen" to monitor progress. "Be aware that redness may be less visible on darker skin tones." — [NICE NG141, rec 1.1.3](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng141/chapter/Recommendations)
- HEE patient leaflet: you can "draw around the edges of it with a pen and note the date". Consent is "vital" if someone else does it. — [HEE Cellulitis leaflet](https://www.hee.nhs.uk/sites/default/files/documents/Cellulitis.pdf)
- NICE advises seeking medical help if "symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly at any time" or do not start to improve **within 2 to 3 days** (of antibiotic treatment). Skin "does take some time to return to normal, and full resolution of symptoms at 5 to 7 days is not expected." — [NICE NG141, rec 1.1.8 and Tables 1–2](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng141/chapter/Recommendations)
- NICE suggests referral or specialist advice for **lymphangitis**, severe illness, infection near the eyes or nose, or infection spreading despite oral antibiotics. Urgent hospital referral is for suspected necrotising fasciitis, sepsis, orbital cellulitis, osteomyelitis or septic arthritis. — [NICE NG141, recs 1.1.13–1.1.14](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng141/chapter/Recommendations)
- Systemic signs need urgent medical review: one or more of malaise, lethargy, loss of appetite, or fever. — [NHS Forth Valley (IWII 2022)](https://nhsforthvalley.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/NHS-Forth-Valley-Wound-Infection-Pathway.pdf)

**Tetanus**
- CDC wound-management schedule, for people with a **completed primary series**:
  - Last dose **under 5 years** ago: no vaccine needed for any wound.
  - Last dose **5 or more years** ago: vaccine recommended for **dirty or major** wounds only.
  - Last dose **10 or more years** ago: vaccine recommended for **all** wounds, including clean and minor ones.
  - **Unknown history, unvaccinated, or incomplete series:** vaccine recommended for all wounds. Tetanus immune globulin (TIG, 250 IU IM) is added for dirty or major wounds (also in HIV or severe immunodeficiency). TIG is never indicated for clean, minor wounds.
  - [CDC, Clinical Guidance for Wound Management to Prevent Tetanus (updated 8 Sep 2026)](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)
- CDC "dirty or major wounds" include penetrating or **puncture** wounds, and wounds contaminated with **dirt, soil, faeces or saliva** (e.g., animal or human bites). They also include wounds with devitalised tissue: **burns, compound fractures, crush injuries, frostbite, necrotic or gangrenous wounds**. — [CDC (2026)](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

### Inferences
- A practical "pen test" for LukaCare: draw along the outer edge of the redness or darkened, warm area, and write the date and time next to it. Take a photo in the same light. Recheck after a few hours and the next day. If the colour or warmth extends past the line, or a red or dark line runs away from the wound, get a same-day check. If this comes with fever or feeling very unwell, go to the emergency department (IGD). This combines NICE/HEE pen-marking with the IWII "spreading" criteria and adapts to brown skin by including warmth and darkening.
- Indonesian context: many minor wounds come from motorbike falls (road dirt), garden or farm soil, nails, and animal bites, which all fall into CDC's "dirty or major" group. Adults unsure of their last tetanus booster should be advised to get checked at a puskesmas. This context is inferred; no Indonesian tetanus-wound protocol was retrieved.

### Gaps
- **Tetanus symptoms and incubation** (e.g., jaw cramping or lockjaw, muscle stiffness and spasms, onset typically days to about 3 weeks after injury) could not be verified because the CDC symptoms page was blocked. These must be sourced before inclusion.
- No retrieved source gave lay guidance on **how often** to recheck a pen line (e.g., every few hours vs daily).
- No Kemenkes or puskesmas guidance on wound urgency triage (when to go to IGD vs puskesmas) was found.

## 6. Indonesian public-health guidance on wound infection signs

### Takeaway
The Indonesian Ministry of Health (Ditjen Kesehatan Lanjutan) has two short public articles from December 2022, written by a hospital nurse. One covers wound types and healing phases, and the other covers infection signs. They use the classic signs rubor (redness), calor (heat), tumor (swelling) and dolor (pain), plus the Morison grading of exudate, redness (≤0.5 cm vs >0.5 cm), swelling, pain and smell. They do **not** mention fever, skin-of-colour considerations, or when or where to seek care, so LukaCare will need to supplement them with NHS, NICE, IWII and CDC guidance.

### Cited Findings
- "Tanda-tanda Infeksi pada Luka" (Signs of Wound Infection) is by Alfin Nurrido, AMd.Kep (RSJ Dr. Radjiman Wediodiningrat Lawang), published 21 Dec 2022 by Ditjen Kesehatan Lanjutan, Kemenkes RI. It lists the classic signs:
  - **Rubor** (redness), from increased blood flow.
  - **Calor** ("rasa panas pada daerah yang mengalami infeksi"), meaning heat in the infected area.
  - **Tumor** (swelling, not cancer).
  - **Dolor** ("Rasa nyeri mengisyaratkan bahwa terjadi gangguan", meaning pain signals that something is wrong). The article warns readers not to ignore pain.
  - [Kemenkes Keslan (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1970/tanda-tanda-infeksi-pada-luka)
- The same article gives Morison (2003) severity grades for each sign:
  - **Exudate:** minimal (non-purulent, ≤¼ dressing), moderate (yellowish, ≤½), heavy (purulent, >½).
  - **Erythema:** mild (none or barely visible), moderate (immediate surroundings, ≤0.5 cm), severe (>0.5 cm).
  - **Oedema:** severe if prominent and "disertai kemerahan" (accompanied by redness).
  - **Pain:** severe if constant or spreading beyond the wound.
  - **Odour:** severe if "bau yang menusuk" (pungent) even with the dressing on.
  - References: a UMY repository thesis and Permenkes No. 27 (2017) on infection prevention and control in health facilities.
  - [Kemenkes Keslan (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1970/tanda-tanda-infeksi-pada-luka)
- "Jenis dan Fase Penyembuhan Luka" (same author and date; page showed about 251,000 views) gives the healing phases as inflammation (to about day 5), proliferation (to about 3 weeks) and maturation (months). The scar goes from red and thick to pale and thin and reaches 80% of normal skin strength. The article cites Ryan (2014), Arisanty (2013) and two university theses. — [Kemenkes Keslan (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
- Alodokter has Indonesian-language consumer articles on dangerous signs of a festering wound ("tanda-tanda luka bernanah yang menjadikannya berbahaya") and on festering scabs ("koreng bernanah"). These were **not read**, so their clinician review status is unverified. — [Alodokter, Tanda-tanda luka bernanah](https://www.alodokter.com/tanda-tanda-luka-bernanah-yang-menjadikannya-berbahaya); [Alodokter, Koreng bernanah](https://www.alodokter.com/koreng-bernanah-ketahui-penyebab-dan-cara-mengobatinya)

### Inferences
- The Kemenkes articles provide officially published Indonesian terms (rubor/kemerahan, calor/rasa panas, tumor/bengkak, dolor/nyeri, eksudat purulen/nanah, bau menusuk) that LukaCare can reuse for consistency. However, they are nurse-authored explainers based largely on textbooks and student theses. They are not formal clinical guidelines and should be presented as supportive rather than authoritative.
- The Kemenkes 0.5 cm redness cut-off (a severity grade) and the IWII 2 cm "spreading" cut-off differ. LukaCare should avoid presenting either as a hard home rule, and should emphasise **change over time** (spreading past a pen line) instead.

### Gaps
- No official Kemenkes, ayosehat.kemkes.go.id or puskesmas **lay first-aid** material was found that tells readers when to go to a puskesmas vs IGD for a wound, or that addresses tetanus boosters after wounds for adults.
- No Indonesian official source was found that addresses how redness looks on brown skin.
- Alodokter and Halodoc pages were not reviewed for clinician authorship. Per the brief, they should be used only if clinician-reviewed.
