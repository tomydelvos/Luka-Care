# Wound Healing Duration by Skin Layer Depth (Acute Wounds), Phases, and Appearance

Scope note: acute, non-burn wounds. Burn sources are used **only** where they are the main published source for partial-thickness depth timelines (flagged as burn-derived each time). Source years are given in brackets. Several primary PMC/PubMed pages (Guo & DiPietro 2010, Winter 1962 abstract) were blocked by a browser-verification wall during research, so their content could not be checked directly (see Gaps).

---

## 1. Phases of acute wound healing: timing and what the wound looks like

### Takeaway
Sources agree on the order: hemostasis (minutes), inflammation (about day 0–5), proliferation/granulation and new skin (about day 3–5 to about week 3), then remodeling/maturation (starts about week 3 and runs for months, up to 12 months for the tissue and up to about 2 years for visible scar fading). Colour changes from red/pink and swollen with clear fluid, to red granulation tissue with new pink, shiny skin, to a red, thick scar that slowly turns pale and thin.

### Cited Findings
**Hemostasis / bleeding and clotting**
- Blood clots "within a few minutes or less," and the clot dries into a scab that protects the tissue underneath. Burns, some puncture wounds and pressure injuries do not bleed. — [MedlinePlus, "Wound care" patient instructions (last reviewed 11 Apr 2026)](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000741.htm)
- Mechanism: vasoconstriction, platelet aggregation and coagulation stop bleeding. No duration given. — [Wallace, Basehore & Zito, "Wound Healing Phases," StatPearls (updated 12 Jun 2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Indonesian description: hemostasis is part of the inflammatory phase. Blood vessels narrow (vasokonstriksi), platelets gather and a fibrin mesh forms. — [Kemenkes Ditjen Yankes, "Jenis dan Fase Penyembuhan Luka" (21 Dec 2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)

**Inflammation**
- Timing is "about 2 to 5 days." The wound is "slightly swollen, red or pink, and tender," and clear fluid oozes out to help clean it. — [MedlinePlus (2026)](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000741.htm)
- Timing is from injury "hingga sekitar hari kelima" (up to about day 5), with vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, and neutrophils and macrophages clearing bacteria and debris. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
- StatPearls says it lasts "several days." Serotonin and histamine increase vascular permeability, and neutrophils clear debris and bacteria. — [StatPearls (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Lay-language source on oozing from scrapes: oozing usually "stops within 4 days." — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise, "How a Scrape Heals" (current as of 9 Nov 2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals)

**Proliferation (tissue growth, granulation, re-epithelialization, contraction)**
- Timing is "about 3 weeks or so." New tissue grows, collagen forms the base, and granulation tissue fills the wound. New skin forms over it, the edges pull inward, and "you may feel itching." — [MedlinePlus (2026)](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000741.htm)
  - Note: this MedlinePlus page attributes collagen production to red blood cells. Standard references attribute it to fibroblasts (StatPearls), so it should not be quoted on that point.
- Timing is from the end of inflammation "hingga sekitar 3 minggu" (up to about 3 weeks). Granulation tissue is described as "berwarna kemerahan" (reddish) and rich in capillaries. Contraction pulls the edges toward the centre, and re-epithelialization forms new epithelium on the surface. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
- StatPearls says this phase lasts "several weeks," with fibroblast collagen deposition beginning about days 5–7. Re-epithelialization begins from the wound edges, and the new epithelium is first a thin layer that thickens over time. — [StatPearls (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)

**Maturation / remodeling**
- Starts around week 3 and "can last up to 12 months." Excess collagen is degraded, and wound contraction peaks around week 3. — [StatPearls (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- The phase can last months ("berbulan-bulan"). The scar changes from "jaringan parut yang kemerahan dan tebal" (reddish, thick scar) to "jaringan parut yang pucat dan tipis" (pale, thin scar). — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
- Scar formation takes "up to about 2 years." After the scab falls off, the area may look "stretched, red, and shiny." Fading "can take as long as 2 years," and "some scars never go away completely." — [MedlinePlus (2026)](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000741.htm)

**Appearance of a healing scrape (lay description, useful for colour cues)**
- When only the outer layers are removed, the scrape "looks pink and raw at first," and healing new skin "sometimes appears yellowish and may be confused with pus." — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise (2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals)
- Without a scab, the scrape stays "moist and pink," may ooze fluid or a little blood, then "the area will turn pink and shiny as the new skin forms." With a scab, a crack over a joint may ooze "clear yellowish to pinkish fluid." — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise (2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals)
- When all skin layers are removed, the wound "looks white at first, and fat cells may be visible." — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise (2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals)
- An abrasion may look lighter than the natural skin tone, red at the borders, speckled, shiny or wet, and may leak pink-to-clear fluid. — [Cleveland Clinic, "Abrasion" (updated 16 Jun 2023)](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25079-abrasion)

**Warning colours and signs (infection or necrosis)**
- Contact a provider for redness or increasing pain, yellow or green pus or excessive clear fluid, black edges (dead tissue), fever ≥100°F (37.7°C) for more than 4 hours, bleeding not stopped after 10 minutes of pressure, or a wound reopening. — [MedlinePlus (2026)](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000741.htm)
- Redness around the wound margins and increasing drainage are warning signs of dehiscence. — [OpenStax Medical-Surgical Nursing §28.2 via LibreTexts (modified 13 Feb 2025)](https://med.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Nursing/Medical-Surgical_Nursing_(OpenStax)/28%3A_Inflammation_and_Healing/28.02%3A_Wound_Healing_Process)

### Inferences
- A defensible lay timeline is: **minutes** (bleeding stops), **day 1–5** (red/pink, swollen, tender, clear oozing), **day 3 to about week 3** (red granular base, new pink/shiny skin from the edges or bottom, itching, scab shrinks and falls off), and **week 3 to 12–24 months** (red/pink scar that slowly turns pale and flat).
- The guidebook should warn that **yellowish new skin** in a healing scrape can look like pus. The difference: pus comes with increasing redness, pain, swelling, smell or fever. This is a key point for a colour-based guide.
- The phases overlap. The day ranges are approximate, and the sources differ slightly: inflammation lasts "about 2–5 days" in MedlinePlus, "up to day 5" in Kemenkes, and "several days" in StatPearls.

### Gaps
- No high-quality source gave validated colour-by-day photo scales for acute wounds in darker skin tones, which matters for Indonesian readers. All colour descriptions found ("red/pink") are framed for lighter skin. Redness and pinkness may look darker, brownish or purplish on darker skin. No source was found to cite for this, so it remains unverified.
- Gurtner 2008 (Nature) and Rodrigues 2019 (Physiol Rev) were not retrieved, so their phase timings could not be cross-checked.

## 2. Healing time by depth of skin layer involved

### Takeaway
Epidermis-only wounds heal in about 3–7 days with no scar. Superficial partial-thickness (upper/papillary dermis) wounds heal in about 7–14 days from surviving hair follicles and sweat glands, usually with little scarring. Deep partial-thickness (reticular dermis) wounds take more than 3 weeks with high hypertrophic-scar risk. Full-thickness wounds into fat cannot regenerate from adnexa and heal only from the edges by granulation and contraction (secondary intention), usually needing closure, grafting or medical care. **Most depth-specific numbers in the literature come from burn and donor-site data, not from mechanical wounds.**

### Cited Findings
**Epidermis only**
- Burn-derived: the epidermis "sloughs off in 3 to 4 days" as keratinocytes regenerate, with minimal damage. — [StatPearls burn-care chapter, "Issues of Concern" section (chapter title, authors and date not shown on page)](https://statpearls.com/sp/al/509/18722/)
- Burn-derived: epidermal burns heal "rapidly, within a week," from undamaged keratinocytes in skin adnexae. — [Papini R., "Management of burn injuries of various depths," BMJ 2004;329:158–160](https://www.bmj.com/content/329/7458/158.full)
- Burn-derived: epidermal injuries heal "within seven days" with "no scar." — [Victorian (Australia) trauma/burns depth poster (page last updated 8 May 2012; publisher not clearly named)](https://trauma.reach.vic.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/poster12-different-burn-depth-characteristic-FINAL.pdf)
- Non-burn equivalent: minor scrapes "usually heal within 3 to 7 days." — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise (2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals)
- Kemenkes grading: "Stadium I" involves only the epidermis. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)

**Superficial partial-thickness (epidermis + upper/papillary dermis)**
- Burn-derived: heals in 10 to 14 days with little to no scar formation. — [StatPearls burn-care chapter (page undated)](https://statpearls.com/sp/al/509/18722/)
- Burn-derived: "Healing is expected within two weeks," with re-epithelialization from keratinocytes in sweat glands and hair follicles. Thin, hairless skin heals more slowly than thick or hairy skin. — [Papini, BMJ 2004](https://www.bmj.com/content/329/7458/158.full)
- Burn-derived: heals in 7–14 days. Most adnexal structures are intact, the wound is pale pink and blanches, and the outcome may be a colour-match defect with low hypertrophic-scar risk. — [Victorian burns depth poster (2012)](https://trauma.reach.vic.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/poster12-different-burn-depth-characteristic-FINAL.pdf)
- Non-burn equivalent: when outer layers are removed, the scrape heals "from the bottom up." Large, deep scrapes "may take up to 1 to 2 weeks or longer." — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise (2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals)
- Kemenkes: "Stadium II" extends into the upper dermis. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)

**Deep partial-thickness (mid/deep or reticular dermis)**
- Burn-derived: heals in "over three weeks" with "noticeable hypertrophic scarring." It may involve hair follicles and sweat/sebaceous glands. — [StatPearls burn-care chapter (page undated)](https://statpearls.com/sp/al/509/18722/)
- Burn-derived: mid/deep dermal injuries, with only the deeper adnexa intact, heal in "over 21 days," with hypertrophic-scar risk "up to 80%." The wound looks blotchy red, or pale/white-based in the deeper forms. Note: the scraped table merged columns, so the mid- vs deep-dermal values were assigned by position. — [Victorian burns depth poster (2012)](https://trauma.reach.vic.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/poster12-different-burn-depth-characteristic-FINAL.pdf)
- Hypertrophic scarring increases when epithelialization is delayed beyond **three weeks**, and wounds still not healing at about **10 days** should be referred for assessment. — [Papini, BMJ 2004](https://www.bmj.com/content/329/7458/158.full)
- Kemenkes: "Stadium III" reaches the lower dermis down to the subcutis. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)

**Full-thickness into subcutaneous fat**
- In a scrape where all skin layers are removed, new skin forms only at the edges and the wound heals "from the edges in to the middle." It "looks white at first, and fat cells may be visible," and "takes longer to heal." No number of days is given. — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise (2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals)
- Burn-derived: full-thickness injuries, with the adnexa destroyed, do "not heal spontaneously," and heal by contraction / secondary intention. — [Victorian burns depth poster (2012)](https://trauma.reach.vic.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/poster12-different-burn-depth-characteristic-FINAL.pdf); healing is "slow; usually requires excision" with extensive scarring — [StatPearls burn-care chapter](https://statpearls.com/sp/al/509/18722/)
- Secondary intention heals "from the 'bottom up' with granulation tissue," with a higher risk of infection and scarring than primary closure. Primary closure (sutures, staples, glue) is "the fastest type of wound closure." — [OpenStax Medical-Surgical Nursing §28.2 (2025)](https://med.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Nursing/Medical-Surgical_Nursing_(OpenStax)/28%3A_Inflammation_and_Healing/28.02%3A_Wound_Healing_Process)
- Wound contraction is much greater in secondary than in primary healing. — [StatPearls Wound Healing Phases (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Partial-thickness wounds "involve the epidermis, dermis, or both," while full-thickness wounds extend through all skin layers. — [OpenStax §28.2 (2025)](https://med.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Nursing/Medical-Surgical_Nursing_(OpenStax)/28%3A_Inflammation_and_Healing/28.02%3A_Wound_Healing_Process)

**Wounds reaching muscle**
- Kemenkes: "Stadium IV" damages all skin layers down to muscle and bone. No healing time is given. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)

### Inferences
- Suggested lay summary, with ranges, for the guidebook. These numbers are synthesized from the sources above, and partial-thickness figures are largely burn-derived:
  - **Epidermis only** (light scrape, blister base): about **3–7 days**, no scar.
  - **Superficial dermis** (scrape that bleeds in pinpoints, pink base): about **7–14 days** (up to about 2 weeks), little or no scar, possibly a temporary colour difference.
  - **Deep dermis**: **more than 3 weeks**, high risk of thick (hypertrophic) scar. Should be seen by a health worker, especially if not healing by about day 10–14.
  - **Into fat (yellow globules visible)**: heals only from the edges over **weeks to months** if left open. Should be assessed early for closure (stitches/glue), ideally within hours.
  - **Into muscle**: always needs medical care. No reliable lay "healing time" exists, because it depends on surgical repair.
- The reason deeper partial-thickness wounds heal more slowly is that fewer hair follicles and sweat glands survive to seed new skin (Papini 2004; StatPearls). This also explains why thin, hairless skin heals more slowly than hairy skin.
- Because burn and mechanical wounds differ (burns have a zone of ongoing tissue injury), applying burn timelines to cuts and scrapes is an approximation. The guidebook should frame these as typical ranges.

### Gaps
- **No retrieved source gave a numeric healing time for full-thickness mechanical wounds left to heal by secondary intention** (e.g., "x weeks per cm"). Textbooks such as Bryant & Nix *Acute and Chronic Wounds* likely contain this but were not accessible.
- **No numeric healing timeline was found for wounds reaching muscle.** Muscle injuries are typically surgical cases, so timelines depend on repair.
- Split-thickness skin-graft donor sites (a standardized partial-thickness wound often reported to heal in about 10–14+ days) were searched, but no full text was retrieved. This is unverified and should not be used without a source.
- The StatPearls burn chapter at statpearls.com/sp/al/509/18722 could not be identified by title, author or date from the page content.

## 3. Healing for common minor wound types

### Takeaway
Minor abrasions heal in about 3–7 days, and large or deep ones in 1–2+ weeks. Sutured cuts have suture removal from 3–5 days (face) to 14–21 days (palms/soles). The wound surface is considered to epithelialize within about 48 hours. Skin glue sloughs off in 5–10 days. Friction blisters usually settle within days, but may last 2+ weeks with ongoing rubbing. For animal bites, primary closure did not raise infection rates in trials. No reliable healing-time data were found for puncture wounds or bites.

### Cited Findings
**Abrasions / scrapes / road rash**
- Minor scrapes "usually heal within 3 to 7 days," while large, deep scrapes "may take up to 1 to 2 weeks or longer." Oozing usually "stops within 4 days." — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise (2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals)
- Small abrasions (under 2 inches, about 5 cm) usually heal within a week, while larger ones can take up to two weeks or more. Small abrasions may heal without scarring, and larger ones can leave a scar or mark. See a provider if there is no healing after one week, if debris is embedded, or for any animal bite or scrape (within 24 hours). — [Cleveland Clinic (2023)](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25079-abrasion)
- Cleveland Clinic classifies abrasions by pattern (linear, grazed, patterned), not by degree. — [Cleveland Clinic (2023)](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25079-abrasion)
- Kemenkes defines "luka lecet (abrasi/ekskoriasis)" as affecting the uppermost skin layer. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)

**Cuts / lacerations: closure, epithelial seal and suture removal**
- Suture or staple removal by site: **face 3–5 days; scalp 7–10; arms 7–10; trunk 10–14; legs 10–14; hands or feet 10–14; palms or soles 14–21 days.** — [Forsch RT, "Essentials of Skin Laceration Repair," Am Fam Physician 2008;78(8):945–951, Table 2](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2008/1015/p945.html)
  - Note: Forsch does not list "over joints" separately. The commonly quoted "over joints 10–14 days" was not confirmed in the retrieved source.
- Noncontaminated wounds have been closed successfully up to **12 hours** after injury. Clean wounds in well-vascularized areas (face, scalp) can sometimes be closed later. Older wounds may be packed for 3–5 days and then closed (delayed primary closure), or left to heal by secondary intention if infected. — [Forsch, AFP 2008](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2008/1015/p945.html)
- Traditionally, the wound is kept clean and dry under a dressing for at least 24 hours, but one study found that uncovering and wetting it after 12 hours did not increase infection. — [Forsch, AFP 2008](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2008/1015/p945.html)
- Tissue adhesive (skin glue) sloughs off spontaneously within **5 to 10 days**. Absorbable sutures usually absorb within **4 to 8 weeks**. Daily antibiotic or white petrolatum ointment is used on wounds not closed with glue, but should be avoided on glued wounds. — [Forsch, AFP 2008](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2008/1015/p945.html)
- Early bathing is defined as within **48 hours** after surgery, "the period during which epithelialisation of the wound occurs." The single trial (n = 857, minor skin excisions) found SSI rates of 8.5% with early bathing vs 8.8% with delayed bathing (RR 0.96; 95% CI 0.62–1.48), which is inconclusive. — [Toon CD et al., Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2015, "Early versus delayed post-operative bathing or showering to prevent wound complications"](https://cochrane.altmetric.com/details/4313742)
- Maximal tensile strength of an incision is reached at about **11–14 weeks**, and the final scar has about **80%** of the original tensile strength. — [StatPearls Wound Healing Phases (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Kemenkes distinguishes "luka sayat (vulnus scissum)," a cut with neat edges, from "luka robek (laserasi)," a tear with irregular edges from blunt trauma. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)

**Friction blisters**
- Fluid collects "just beneath the outermost layer of skin" (an epidermal-level injury). Blisters "typically drain on their own within days," but may last "two weeks or longer" if pressure continues. New skin forms beneath and the blistered skin then peels away. Advice is not to pierce or cut away the roof, and to see a doctor for significant redness, non-clear drainage or fever, or if the person has diabetes and blisters don't heal. — [Harvard Health Publishing, "Friction Blisters" (updated 21 Nov 2023; archived)](https://www.health.harvard.edu/a_to_z/friction-blisters-a-to-z)

**Animal bites**
- In a 2014 meta-analysis of 4 RCTs (N = 998), primary closure of dog bites did not increase infection: 7.0% closed vs 7.6% not closed (RR 0.93; 95% CI 0.6–1.4). In a 2000 case series of 145 mammalian bites closed primarily, infection was 5.5%. No healing-time figures are reported. — [Cade, Low & Head, "Primary Closure of Animal Bites," Am Fam Physician 2018;97(9)](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2018/0501/od2)
- Any animal bite or scrape should be seen by a provider within 24 hours. — [Cleveland Clinic (2023)](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25079-abrasion)

**Puncture wounds**
- Some puncture wounds do not bleed. — [MedlinePlus (2026)](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000741.htm)

### Inferences
- For a lay guidebook, these points can be stated with ranges: "a light scrape: about 3–7 days; a big or deep scrape: 1–2 weeks or more"; "stitches on the face come out after about 3–5 days, on the arms/scalp 7–10 days, on the trunk/legs/hands/feet 10–14 days, on the palms/soles 14–21 days"; "a cut that may need stitches should be seen within hours (ideally within about 12 hours)."
- The 24–48-hour "epithelial seal" figure is supported only as the defined window in the Cochrane review, not as a measured outcome. It is best phrased as "the wound surface usually closes over within about 1–2 days after proper closure."

### Gaps
- **No healing-time data were found for puncture wounds** or for animal bites, either sutured or left open.
- The more recent AFP laceration article (2017 or later, referenced on the 2008 page) was not retrieved, so updated suture-removal tables were not cross-checked.
- No source was found for "over joints 10–14 days" specifically.

## 4. Moist vs dry healing

### Takeaway
Classic animal work (Winter 1962, pig) found that occlusion **roughly doubled the rate of re-epithelialization** compared with air-exposed, scabbed wounds. Human experiments (Hinman & Maibach 1963) and later reviews support faster epithelialization, less pain, and infection rates that are often lower under occlusion. However, the exact human percentage gain could not be verified from accessible text, and one lay source states that whether a scab forms does not change healing time.

### Cited Findings
- In the domestic pig, "an occlusive dressing doubles the rate of wound re-epithelization" compared with air exposure. This is attributed to Winter (Nature 1962;193:293) and Winter & Scales (Nature 1963;197:91). — [Hinman CD & Maibach H, "Effect of Air Exposure and Occlusion on Experimental Human Skin Wounds," Nature 1963;200:377–378 (abstract)](https://www.nature.com/articles/200377a0)
- Hinman & Maibach "report parallel studies performed in man." The human figures are behind a paywall and were not retrieved. — [Hinman & Maibach, Nature 1963](https://www.nature.com/articles/200377a0)
- Winter's 1962 pig study found that wounds "healed faster if kept moist" than wounds left to dry and scab, as measured by migration of new epithelium. — [Wikipedia, "George D. Winter" (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_D._Winter)
- A reevaluation of the experimental studies found that occlusive dressings let wounds re-epithelialize "more rapidly," that "infection rates with occlusion are frequently less than those with non-occlusive dressings," and that wound pain decreases. No pooled magnitude was given in the accessible text. — [Zhai H & Maibach HI, "Effect of Occlusion and Semi-occlusion on Experimental Skin Wound Healing: A Reevaluation," *Wounds* 2007](https://www.hmpgloballearningnetwork.com/site/wounds/article/7894)
- Too much moisture macerates wound edges, while too little inhibits cell activity and promotes eschar. — [OpenStax §28.2 (2025)](https://med.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Nursing/Medical-Surgical_Nursing_(OpenStax)/28%3A_Inflammation_and_Healing/28.02%3A_Wound_Healing_Process)
- **Contrasting lay statement:** "Whether a scab forms does not change healing time or scarring." — [HealthLinkBC/Healthwise (2022)](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/how-scrape-heals). This conflicts with the experimental occlusion literature above.
- Practical care in lay sources: wash with soap and water, apply ointment, and cover with a bandage changed daily until healed. — [Cleveland Clinic (2023)](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25079-abrasion); petrolatum or antibiotic ointment daily on non-glued wounds — [Forsch, AFP 2008](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2008/1015/p945.html)

### Inferences
- A safe guidebook message: "Keeping a clean wound covered and slightly moist (e.g., with petroleum jelly and a bandage) helps new skin grow faster and hurts less than letting it dry into a hard scab. In animal studies it was about twice as fast. Don't make it soggy." The size of the human benefit is uncertain.
- The Healthwise statement may reflect a lay simplification. The report writer should present the moist-healing benefit as the scientific consensus, while noting that small, simple scrapes heal well either way.

### Gaps
- Specific human trial magnitudes (e.g., percentage reduction in time to epithelialization in RCTs of occlusive vs gauze dressings for abrasions or donor sites) were not retrieved. The Winter 1962 original and Hinman & Maibach full text were behind captcha or paywall.
- No systematic review (e.g., Cochrane) on dressings for simple traumatic abrasions was retrieved.

## 5. Scar maturation: how long new skin stays pink/red and when it fades

### Takeaway
Scar remodeling begins around week 3 and the tissue continues to mature for up to about 12 months (StatPearls). Visible fading can take up to about 2 years (MedlinePlus). The scar goes from red/thick to pale/thin (Kemenkes). Final strength is only about 80% of normal skin, and maximal incision strength is reached at about 11–14 weeks.

### Cited Findings
- Maturation starts around week 3 and can last up to 12 months. The final scar has about 80% of the original tensile strength, and maximal incision tensile strength is reached at about 11–14 weeks. — [StatPearls (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Scar formation takes "up to about 2 years." The new scar looks "stretched, red, and shiny," usually fades over time ("as long as 2 years"), and some scars never fade completely. Deeper wounds are more likely to scar. — [MedlinePlus (2026)](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000741.htm)
- The scar goes from "kemerahan dan tebal" (reddish and thick) to "pucat dan tipis" (pale and thin), with scar strength about 80% of normal skin. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
- Superficial dermal injuries may leave a colour-match defect, and deeper dermal injuries carry hypertrophic-scar risk of up to 80% (burn data). — [Victorian burns depth poster (2012)](https://trauma.reach.vic.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/poster12-different-burn-depth-characteristic-FINAL.pdf)
- Hypertrophic scarring rises when epithelialization takes longer than 3 weeks, and hypertrophic scars occur in 60% of burnt children under 5 (burn context). — [Papini, BMJ 2004](https://www.bmj.com/content/329/7458/158.full)
- **Lower-quality source (commercial hospital blog; colour timeline not referenced):** red, swollen and tender at days 0–6; pink, slightly raised line at weeks 1–4; red, raised and firm at weeks 4–8 ("often the worst-looking phase"); redness begins fading at months 3–6; pink fading toward pale at months 6–12; pale, flat and soft at months 12–18. — [Acibadem International blog, Dr. Şule Eren (1 Sep 2026)](https://acibademinternational.com/blog/scar-healing-stages-the-12-18-month-journey-from-red-line-to-faded-mark/). Use only as illustrative and do not cite as evidence.

### Inferences
- Guidebook phrasing: "New skin and new scars are pink or red at first. This is normal and is not infection if there is no pain, swelling, pus or fever. Scars often look their reddest and thickest around 1–2 months, then slowly fade and flatten over 6–12 months, and can keep improving for up to about 2 years. A scar is never quite as strong as the original skin (about 80%)."
- The "reddest at 1–2 months" point rests only on the low-quality blog together with the general StatPearls/Kemenkes descriptions, so it should be phrased cautiously.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source was retrieved that quantifies the duration of scar erythema in mechanical (non-burn) wounds, or that addresses post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in darker skin. Scars in Indonesian skin types often become darker (brown) rather than staying pink. This is clinically plausible, but no source was retrieved to cite.
- NHS "Scars" pages were not retrieved.

## 6. Factors that prolong healing and the threshold for a non-healing/chronic wound

### Takeaway
Diabetes, smoking, malnutrition, infection/biofilm, poor blood flow/hypoxia, edema, pressure, immunosuppression, older age, obesity, certain drugs (steroids, NSAIDs, etc.) and venous disease all slow healing. A wound not healed in about **4 weeks / 30 days** is considered chronic. For burns, about 10 days without healing triggers referral, and for an abrasion, Cleveland Clinic says to see a provider if there is no healing after one week.

### Cited Findings
- Factors that impair healing:
  - Primary: hypoxia, bacterial colonization, ischemia, reperfusion injury, altered cellular response, and collagen synthesis defects.
  - Systemic: diabetes, smoking, and malnutrition.
  - Local: pressure, edema, infection, maceration, and dehydration.
  - Biofilm creates low oxygen and low pH and acts as a barrier.
  - Drugs: anticonvulsants, steroids, antibiotics, angiogenesis inhibitors, and NSAIDs.
  — [StatPearls (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Patient factors include age, immune status, perfusion, smoking, comorbidities (diabetes directly linked) and nutritional deficiencies. Deficiencies in vitamin C, zinc, iron and vitamin A are linked to infection and reduced tensile strength. Vasoconstriction from pain, cold, fear, nicotine, beta-blockers and hypovolemia also slows healing. — [OpenStax §28.2 (2025)](https://med.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Nursing/Medical-Surgical_Nursing_(OpenStax)/28%3A_Inflammation_and_Healing/28.02%3A_Wound_Healing_Process)
- A chronic wound is "a sore that takes longer than 4 weeks to heal," while an acute wound heals properly, "usually within around 4 weeks." Risk factors include uncontrolled diabetes (diabetic foot ulcers), obesity, heart failure, lung disease, anaemia, varicose veins, prior DVT, a weakened immune system (HIV, uncontrolled diabetes, cancer), dermatitis, dry skin, prior radiotherapy, peripheral neuropathy and limb oedema. — [healthdirect Australia, "Chronic wounds" (reviewed Feb 2025)](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/chronic-wounds)
- The CMS definition: a wound not closed or progressing within "thirty days" is chronic (citing Baranoski & Ayello 2020). — [OpenStax §28.2 (2025)](https://med.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Nursing/Medical-Surgical_Nursing_(OpenStax)/28%3A_Inflammation_and_Healing/28.02%3A_Wound_Healing_Process)
- Kemenkes: acute wounds have "penyembuhan fisiologis 0–21 hari" (physiological healing over 0–21 days), while chronic wounds last long or recur, e.g., ulcers. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
- For burns, wounds still not healing at about 10 days should be referred, and delay beyond 3 weeks raises scar risk. — [Papini, BMJ 2004](https://www.bmj.com/content/329/7458/158.full)
- For an abrasion, see a provider if there is no healing after one week. — [Cleveland Clinic (2023)](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/25079-abrasion)
- People with diabetes whose blisters don't heal should see a doctor. — [Harvard Health (2023)](https://www.health.harvard.edu/a_to_z/friction-blisters-a-to-z)

### Inferences
- A layered "when to worry" rule for lay readers:
  - (a) **Any time**: signs of infection (spreading redness, pus, smell, fever), black edges, or bleeding beyond 10 minutes.
  - (b) **About 1 week**: a simple scrape is not visibly improving.
  - (c) **About 10–14 days**: a partial-thickness wound is not closed. This is when scar risk starts rising.
  - (d) **Beyond about 4 weeks**: the wound counts as chronic and needs professional wound care.
- People with diabetes, the elderly, smokers, the malnourished, the immunosuppressed and anyone with a **lower-leg/foot wound** should seek care earlier. Location-specific delay (lower leg/foot) is clinically recognized, and healthdirect highlights diabetic foot ulcers, but no retrieved source quantified delay by body location.

### Gaps
- Guo & DiPietro 2010 (J Dent Res 89(3):219–229, PMC2903966) could not be read (PMC captcha), so its quantitative findings on age, smoking, diabetes, stress, alcohol and obesity were not extracted.
- No retrieved source quantified how much slower healing is in older adults, smokers or people with diabetes for simple acute wounds.
- healthdirect's general "wounds" page could not be fetched (permission timeout). Only the chronic-wounds page was retrieved.

## 7. Indonesian clinical guidance and Indonesian-language sources

### Takeaway
The Ministry of Health (Kemenkes, Ditjen Pelayanan Kesehatan) has a 2022 lay article in Indonesian. It gives the three-phase model (inflamasi to about day 5, proliferasi to about 3 weeks, maturasi lasting months), a stadium I–IV depth grading, Indonesian wound-type terms, and colour descriptions of granulation tissue and scars. No formal Kemenkes clinical guideline (PNPK) on acute wound healing times was found.

### Cited Findings
- Article: "Jenis dan Fase Penyembuhan Luka" by Alfin Nurrido, AMd.Kep (RS Jiwa Dr. Radjiman Wediodiningrat Lawang), published 21 Dec 2022 on the Kemenkes Ditjen Yankes site. — [Kemenkes (2022)](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/view_artikel/1969/jenis-dan-fase-penyembuhan-luka)
  - Wound types:
    - Luka terbuka (open wounds): luka lecet (abrasi/ekskoriasis), luka sayat (vulnus scissum), luka robek (laserasi), luka tusuk (stab/puncture), luka gigitan (bite), luka tembak (gunshot) and luka bakar (combustio).
    - Luka tertutup (closed wounds): memar (contusio) and hematoma.
  - By duration: luka akut ("terjadi kurang dari 5 hari," with physiological healing over 0–21 days) vs luka kronik (ulcers).
  - Depth grading:
    - Stadium I: epidermis only
    - Stadium II: into upper dermis
    - Stadium III: lower dermis to subcutis
    - Stadium IV: all layers to muscle and bone
  - Phases:
    - Inflamasi: to about day 5
    - Proliferasi/fibroplasia: to about 3 weeks, with "jaringan granulasi" described as "berwarna kemerahan"
    - Maturasi/remodelling: months; the scar goes from "kemerahan dan tebal" to "pucat dan tipis," with strength about 80% of normal skin
- Search results also returned Indonesian academic sources (Poltekkes, university repositories and journals) describing the healing phases, e.g., [Poltekkes Malang repository Bab 2](https://repository.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/2135/3/Bab2.docx.pdf) and [Universitas Setia Budi eprint Bab 2](https://eprints.setiabudi.ac.id/id/eprint/498/5/BAB%202.pdf). These are student theses/KTI and were **not** opened or verified.

### Inferences
- The Kemenkes article is useful for **official Indonesian terminology** (luka lecet, luka sayat, luka robek, luka tusuk, jaringan granulasi, fase inflamasi/proliferasi/maturasi, stadium I–IV). Its timings agree with international sources: inflammation about 5 days, proliferation about 3 weeks, maturation months, and scar strength about 80%.
- The Kemenkes phrase "luka akut: terjadi kurang dari 5 hari" is ambiguous. It seems to describe time since injury, not healing time, so it should not be quoted as a healing duration. The "0–21 hari" physiological healing window is more usable.
- The Kemenkes stadium I–IV grading resembles pressure-injury staging and maps onto the depth categories in Section 2: I = epidermal, II = superficial dermal, III = deep dermal to subcutis, IV = muscle/bone.

### Gaps
- No Kemenkes PNPK (national clinical practice guideline) or Buku Saku on acute wound or abrasion healing times was found.
- Indonesian surgical textbooks (e.g., Sjamsuhidajat–De Jong *Buku Ajar Ilmu Bedah*) are standard references for wound-healing phases in Indonesia but were not accessible online, and their figures could not be verified.
- Indonesian journal and thesis sources from the search were not opened, so their content is unverified.
