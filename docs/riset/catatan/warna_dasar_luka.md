# Wound Bed Color Classification and Healing Stage / Expected Healing Time (Warna Dasar Luka & Waktu Penyembuhan)

Scope note for the report writer: Almost all of the color-classification literature comes from chronic wounds (pressure ulcers, leg ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers) and from nursing/tissue-viability practice. Evidence specific to minor acute wounds (abrasions, cuts, small burns) is thin. Burn-depth literature is the best acute-wound source that links color and skin layer to healing time. Sources are tagged with year and, where relevant, a quality note.

Indonesian color terms used in Indonesian nursing teaching (Arisanty 2013, cited in an Indonesian Poltekkes thesis): **hitam** (black = jaringan nekrosis), **kuning** (yellow = slough, "nanah beku"), **merah** (red = jaringan granulasi), **merah muda** (pink = epitelisasi). Suggested lay terms that do not appear in the sources: **hijau** (green), **ungu/kebiruan/gelap** (purple/dusky), **merah pucat** (pale red).

## Q1. What are the established color classification systems and frameworks, who proposed them, and when?

### Takeaway
The basic system is the three-color **Red-Yellow-Black (RYB)** concept. It is usually credited to Cuzzell (1988), with related color systems from Stotts (1990), Krasner (1995) and Lorentzen et al. (1999). The UK **Wound Healing Continuum (WHC)** (Gray, White, Cooper et al., Wounds UK, mid-2000s) extended RYB into a 7-step gradient (black, black/yellow, yellow, yellow/red, red, red/pink, pink) and added separate continua for infection and exudate. The broader frameworks (TIME/TIMERS, Sibbald's Wound Bed Preparation) are not color systems. They use tissue type, the "T" in TIME, as one part of a full assessment.

### Cited Findings
**RYB origin**
- One study guide says Cuzzell developed the RYB system "in 1988". Its colors and goals are **Red** (healthy, vascularised granulation or pale pink epithelial tissue, so PROTECT), **Yellow** (slough, soft non-viable debris, purulent exudate, so DEBRIDE / absorb exudate / manage bioburden) and **Black** (hard, dry, leathery eschar that blocks granulation, so DEBRIDE). Quality note: this is an exam-prep study guide, not a primary source. — [Open Exam Prep, ABWM/CWCA study guide, n.d.](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/abwm-cwca/assessment-and-diagnosis/measure-framework-ryb)
- Gray et al. (2006, Wounds UK 2(2)) cite earlier color-based systems: **Cuzzell 1988, Stotts 1990, Krasner 1995, Lorentzen et al. 1999**. These systems held that "black and yellow wounds should be debrided." — [Gray D, Cooper P, McGuffog J, Russell F, Stringfellow S, Kingsley A, White R. "A clinical audit of a TVS using applied wound management", Wounds UK 2006;2(2)](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)

**Dutch WCS variant (RYB as a national data standard)**
- The Netherlands' national health information model (zib "Wound") uses the **WCS (Woundcare Consultant Society, WCS Kenniscentrum Wondzorg)** red-yellow-black classification. Red = "granulerend weefsel" (granulation tissue). **Yellow = "met infectieverschijnselen of fibrinebeslag"** (signs of infection OR a fibrin layer). Black = "met necrose" (necrosis). WCS is used for the "T" (tissue) in TIME. It applies to surgical wounds, trauma wounds, diabetic foot ulcers and venous ulcers, but not to oncological ulcers. — [Nictiz zib Wound v3.0 (2016)](https://zibs.nl/wiki/Wound-v3.0(2016EN))
  - Note: in the WCS version, "yellow" covers both slough/fibrin and infection. Anglophone RYB treats yellow as slough and handles infection separately.

**Wound Healing Continuum (WHC), UK**
- The WHC colors run left to right: **black, black/yellow, yellow, yellow/red, red, red/pink, pink**. Treatment rule: "Identification of the tissue furthest to the left of the continuum will determine treatment objectives." Black, black/yellow and yellow wounds need debridement. Yellow/red needs debridement plus promotion of granulation. Red/pink indicates granulation and re-epithelialisation. — [Gray et al., Wounds UK 2006](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)
- In that paper, **green is not on the WHC itself**. Infection is handled by a separate **Wound Infection Continuum** (colonised, then critical colonisation, local infection, spreading infection). Exudate is handled by a **Wound Exudate Continuum**. — [Gray et al., Wounds UK 2006](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)
- The authors warn that color-only coding is "an over simplification" (citing White 2003), because infection and exudate must also be assessed. — [Gray et al., Wounds UK 2006](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)

**Indonesian teaching (four colors)**
- Indonesian nursing teaching follows Arisanty (2013), "Konsep Dasar Manajemen Perawatan Luka":
  - **hitam**: "jaringan nekrosis (mati)", tends to be hard and dry; must be removed; risk of deep tissue injury.
  - **kuning**: "jaringan nekrosis yang lunak berbentuk seperti nanah beku" (slough), with abundant exudate.
  - **merah**: "jaringan granulasi dengan vaskularisasi yang baik", the clinical goal until the wound closes.
  - **merah muda**: epithelialisation progressing to maturation; fragile, must be protected.
  - Source quality: a student thesis (KTI) chapter quoting the textbook, not the textbook itself. — [Poltekkes Kemenkes Malang, KTI BAB 2 (citing Arisanty 2013)](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)

**Wound Bed Preparation (Sibbald)**
- The 2021 update of Sibbald et al.'s **Wound Bed Preparation (WBP)** has 10 statements:
  1. Treat the cause.
  2. Address patient-centred concerns.
  3. Determine healability: healable, maintenance or non-healable.
  4. Monitor history/examination.
  5. Debride, with adequate pain control.
  6. Treat infection/inflammation.
  7. Manage moisture.
  8. Assess rate of healing.
  9. Address the edge effect.
  10. Secure organisational support.
- The 2021 paper does not use a color scheme. — [Sibbald RG, Elliott JA, Persaud-Jaimangal R, et al. "Wound Bed Preparation 2021", Adv Skin Wound Care 2021; reprinted WCET Journal 2022;42(1):16-28](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/wcetcn/volume-42-number-1/wound-bed-preparation)
- WBP 2021 healability classification: healable is about two-thirds of community wounds; maintenance about one-quarter; non-healable about 5–10%. — [Sibbald et al. 2021/2022](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/wcetcn/volume-42-number-1/wound-bed-preparation)

**TIMERS**
- TIMERS citation: Atkin L, Bućko Z, Conde Montero E, Cutting K, Moffatt C, Probst A, Romanelli M, Schultz GS, Tettelbach W. "Implementing TIMERS: the race against hard-to-heal wounds." J Wound Care 2019;28(Suppl 3):S1–S49. The summary describes hard-to-heal wounds as "those that do not close after care for up to a year or more." — [LINK for Wound Healing summary page (Mölnlycke-hosted; industry site)](https://www.linkforwoundhealing.info/en/scientific-content/2019/implementing-timers-the-race-against-hard-to-heal-wounds)

**MEASURE**
- MEASURE (Measure, Exudate, Appearance [tissue types as % totalling 100%], Suffering, Undermining, Re-evaluate, Edge) is another assessment acronym. It records tissue color as percentages. For mixed wounds, the priority order is black, then yellow, then red: address the worst component first. — [Open Exam Prep study guide (low-quality source)](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/abwm-cwca/assessment-and-diagnosis/measure-framework-ryb)

### Inferences
- For a lay guidebook, the most defensible simple model is **four colors (hitam, kuning, merah, merah muda)**, as taught in Indonesia (Arisanty 2013) and matching the WHC's endpoints. Infection signs (green pus, smell, spreading redness/warmth) should sit in a **separate "warning signs" box**, not be treated as a wound "color stage". This mirrors how the WHC keeps infection on its own continuum.
- The "worst color wins" rule (WHC "furthest to the left"; MEASURE "black, then yellow, then red") turns easily into lay advice. A wound with any black or thick yellow tissue is not yet in the "healing well" stage.
- The Dutch WCS shows that "yellow" is used inconsistently across systems: slough in some, possible infection in others. The guidebook should define yellow clearly (sticky yellow/cream layer = slough) and state that yellow is not automatically infection.

### Gaps
- Not verified from fetched text: the original Cuzzell 1988 article (commonly cited as Cuzzell JZ, "The new RYB color code", Am J Nurs 1988), Stotts 1990, Krasner 1995, and the original 2005 WHC paper (Gray D, White R, Cooper P, Kingsley A, Applied Wound Management, Wounds UK). Those details come from secondary citations, and the Cuzzell article title is from the researcher's background knowledge, so it needs checking.
- **TIME framework (Schultz GS et al., Wound Repair Regen 2003;11 Suppl 1:S1–28)**: the PubMed fetch failed (permission timeout), so the acronym and origin were not verified from source text. Zibs.nl confirms only that the "T" (tissue) of TIME exists. For verification: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12654015/
- **TIMERS acronym expansion** (Tissue, Infection/Inflammation, Moisture, Edge, Regeneration/Repair, Social factors) was not shown on the fetched summary page. Verify in the full text: https://www.magonlinelibrary.com/doi/full/10.12968/jowc.2019.28.Sup3a.S1
- Sibbald's original WBP (2000) was not fetched.
- The extended five-color scheme (black/yellow/red/pink/**green**) popularised in Grey, Enoch & Harding, "ABC of wound healing: Wound assessment", BMJ 2006 could not be fetched (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1414680/ timed out). No verified primary source was found that includes green or purple as an official wound-bed color category.
- No Kemenkes guideline or InETNA/WOCARE Indonesia document on wound color was found in this session.

## Q2. For each color, what tissue is it, what does it look and feel like, what healing phase does it indicate, and what skin layer is involved?

### Takeaway
- **Black (hitam):** dead tissue (necrosis/eschar). Healing has not started at that spot.
- **Yellow (kuning):** soft dead tissue/fibrin (slough). Debris is still being cleared, so the wound is in a late inflammatory or "cleaning" state.
- **Red (merah):** granulation tissue. This is the proliferative phase and means the wound is filling in.
- **Pink (merah muda):** new epithelium. This is late proliferative into early maturation, and the wound is closing.

Skin depth is not part of any color system. Indirectly, granulation (red) implies tissue loss into or through the dermis, and black eschar often signals deep, full-thickness damage. Burn-depth data link color to layer most directly for acute wounds.

### Cited Findings
**BLACK (hitam): necrosis / eschar**
- Black tissue is "jaringan nekrosis (mati)" with a "kecenderungan keras dan kering" (tends to be hard and dry). It is at risk of being a deep tissue injury, and the tissue must be removed. — [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)
- Black is "hard, dry, leathery eschar that prevents granulation". Exception: stable, dry, intact eschar with no redness or fluctuance on heels or ischaemic limbs should NOT be debrided. — [Open Exam Prep study guide (low-quality source)](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/abwm-cwca/assessment-and-diagnosis/measure-framework-ryb)
- In darker skin, "Severe DFUs in patients with dark skin tones may present with black or brown eschar overlying the ulcer." — [Dhoonmoon L, Nair HKR, Abbas Z et al. Wound Care and Skin Tone: Signs, symptoms and terminology for all skin tones. Wounds International 2023](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/MULTI23_ME_SkinTones_WINT-web.pdf)
- Burns: full-thickness (third-degree) burns involve the epidermis and dermis and extend into subcutaneous tissue. They are "leathery, stiff, and dry", do not blanch, and are painless because the nerves are destroyed. — [StatPearls, Burn Classification (Warby R, Maani C; updated 26 Sep 2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)

**YELLOW (kuning): slough / fibrin**
- Yellow is "jaringan nekrosis yang lunak berbentuk seperti nanah beku" (soft necrotic tissue shaped like frozen pus), called slough. It has much exudate. — [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)
- Yellow is "fibrous slough, soft non-viable debris, purulent exudate, or liquefying yellow/tan tissue." — [Open Exam Prep (low-quality source)](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/abwm-cwca/assessment-and-diagnosis/measure-framework-ryb)
- In the Dutch WCS, yellow = "with signs of infection or fibrin layer". — [Nictiz zib Wound v3.0 (2016)](https://zibs.nl/wiki/Wound-v3.0(2016EN))
- Black or yellow necrosis does not necessarily mean infection. Under NPUAP, a pressure injury whose base is ≥50% yellow or black necrosis is "unstageable": the depth cannot be seen. — [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)
- WBP 2021 defines debridement as the way "to remove slough, debris or foreign substances that may facilitate infection". — [Sibbald et al. 2021/2022](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/wcetcn/volume-42-number-1/wound-bed-preparation)

**RED (merah): granulation tissue**
- Red is "jaringan granulasi dengan vaskularisasi yang baik" (well-vascularised granulation tissue) and is the clinical goal until the wound closes. **Pale red ("merah pucat") may indicate a biofilm layer.** — [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)
- Red is "clean, healthy, vascularized granulation tissue … with low infection risk", and the goal is to PROTECT it and keep it moist. Harsh antiseptics should be avoided. — [Open Exam Prep (low-quality source)](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/abwm-cwca/assessment-and-diagnosis/measure-framework-ryb)
- Biology:
  - Granulation is not a discrete event; it runs in the background.
  - By days 5–7, fibroblasts lay down new collagen and glycosaminoglycans that form the core of the wound.
  - The proliferative phase lasts "several weeks".
  - Wound contraction (myofibroblasts) peaks around week 3 and is much greater in secondary-intention healing.
  - [StatPearls, Wound Healing Phases (Wallace HA, Basehore BM, Zito PM; updated 12 Jun 2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Burns: after a superficial partial-thickness burn blister is removed, the bed is **red or pink and blanches with pressure**. These burns involve the papillary (superficial) dermis and are painful. Deep partial-thickness burns (reticular dermis) have a **mottled** bed that blanches sluggishly. — [StatPearls, Burn Classification (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)

**PINK (merah muda): epithelialisation**
- Pink shows epithelialisation progressing well towards maturation. The tissue is fragile and needs protection. — [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)
- The WHC places "red/pink" and "pink" at the healing end of the continuum (granulation and re-epithelialisation). — [Gray et al., Wounds UK 2006](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)
- Epithelialisation starts around days 5–7, with cells migrating from the wound edges. It begins as a thin superficial layer that thickens and becomes more durable over time. — [StatPearls, Wound Healing Phases (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Superficial (first-degree) burns involve the epidermis only. They are pink to red and dry, with no blisters. — [StatPearls, Burn Classification (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)

**GREEN / PURPLE / DUSKY (other colors)**
- Green belongs to the separate Wound Exudate Continuum in the WHC literature, not to the tissue continuum. — [Gray et al., Wounds UK 2006](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)
- "Dependent rubor", a dusky red colour of the foot that blanches when the leg is raised, is a vascular (arterial insufficiency) sign in WBP 2021, not a wound-bed category. — [Sibbald et al. 2021/2022](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/wcetcn/volume-42-number-1/wound-bed-preparation)
- Infected hypergranulation "may look red to dark brown and [be] friable". — [Oxford Community Tissue Viability Service, Hypergranulation Pathway v3, 27 Nov 2023](https://oxfordhealth.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/51/2024/01/Hypergranulation-Pathway-FINAL.pdf)

**Phase timeline (to map onto colors)**
- Indonesian teaching:
  - **Inflammation**: day 0 to day 3–5.
  - **Proliferation (fibroplasia)**: day 2 to about day 24, or "until about the end of week 3"; includes cleaning, granulation and epithelialisation.
  - **Maturation/remodelling**: day 21 to 1–2 years.
  - New skin regains only about **80%** of its original quality.
  - [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013 and Sjamsuhidajat & de Jong 2017](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)
- StatPearls:
  - Inflammation lasts "several days".
  - Proliferation lasts "several weeks".
  - Remodelling runs from about week 3 to 12 months.
  - Maximal tensile strength of an incision comes at about 11–14 weeks, reaching only about **80%** of the original.
  - [StatPearls, Wound Healing Phases (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)

### Inferences
Color-to-phase-to-layer mapping (synthesised by the researcher; color-to-layer links are inferred, not stated in any color system):

| Color (ID) | Tissue | Look and feel | Healing phase | Typical depth / layer (inferred) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hitam (black) | Necrosis / eschar | Hard, dry, leathery; brown to black; no pain if full-thickness | Healing blocked; dead tissue must separate or be removed first | Often deep: full-thickness (into dermis or subcutaneous fat or deeper); depth hidden ("unstageable") |
| Kuning (yellow) | Slough / fibrin (soft dead tissue) | Soft, sticky, stringy, creamy/yellow-tan; wet; adherent | Inflammatory / cleaning phase; granulation underneath held back | Usually partial- to full-thickness; depth often hidden |
| Merah (red) | Granulation tissue | Moist, bumpy/granular, healthy red, bleeds only slightly | Proliferative phase (fills from about day 3–7 onward) | Implies tissue loss into or through the dermis, so the wound must fill from the base (secondary intention) |
| Merah muda (pink) | New epithelium | Thin, shiny, smooth, fragile, pink; grows in from edges or islands | Late proliferative into maturation (epithelialisation from about day 5–7) | Epidermis regrowing; for superficial / partial-thickness wounds this may be the main healing route |

- Abrasions (lecet) and superficial partial-thickness injuries mostly heal by epithelialisation (pink) from the edges and the remaining skin appendages, often with little visible red granulation. Deep wounds (luka dalam, full-thickness) go through red granulation first. This matches the burn-depth data, but no source fetched here states it directly for abrasions.
- For lay readers, "merah segar, lembap" (fresh red, moist) and "merah muda mengkilap dari tepi" (shiny pink from the edges) can be described as good signs. "Hitam keras" (hard black) and "kuning lengket" (sticky yellow) mean the wound still needs cleaning or a professional assessment.

### Gaps
- No fetched source gave a validated mapping of each RYB color to a specific skin layer. The layer column above is inference from burn-depth data and general wound biology.
- The classic textbook description of healthy granulation ("beefy red, moist, granular/cobblestone") could not be verified from fetched text; the BMJ ABC article and the Bryant & Nix textbook were not accessible. Only Arisanty's "vaskularisasi yang baik" and the study guide's "healthy, vascularized" were captured.
- Green (Pseudomonas) and purple/dusky as wound-bed colors: no verified primary source was obtained in this session.

## Q3. What does the literature say about time spent in each color/phase and time to closure?

### Takeaway
No source gives validated "days per color". Evidence comes from phase timelines and acute burn data:
- Granulation and epithelialisation start around days 3–7.
- Acute wounds generally heal in **4–6 weeks**; failure in that window defines a chronic wound.
- Superficial burns heal in **5–10 days**; superficial partial-thickness burns in **2–3 weeks**; full-thickness burns take **more than 8 weeks** and need surgery.
- For chronic wounds, the most-cited predictor is percentage area reduction (PAR): a wound that is **not 20–40% smaller by week 4** (≥50% for diabetic foot ulcers) is unlikely to heal by week 12.

### Cited Findings
**Phase timing / acute wounds**
- Wounds generally heal in 4–6 weeks; wounds that fail to heal in that time are considered chronic. Epithelialisation and fibroblast activity are under way by days 5–7. Remodelling runs from about week 3 to 12 months. — [StatPearls, Wound Healing Phases (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)
- Indonesian teaching: inflammation day 0–3/5; proliferation (granulation and epithelialisation) from day 2 to about day 24 / end of week 3; maturation from day 21 to 1–2 years. — [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013; Sjamsuhidajat & de Jong 2017](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)

**Burns (best acute data linking color to healing time)**
- **Superficial / first-degree** (epidermis only; pink-red, dry, no blisters): heals in **5–10 days** without scarring.
- **Superficial partial-thickness** (papillary dermis; blisters; red/pink bed that blanches; painful): heals in about **2–3 weeks** with minimal scarring.
- **Deep partial-thickness** (reticular dermis; mottled bed, sluggish blanching): can heal without surgery but more slowly, and scarring is unavoidable.
- **Full-thickness** (into subcutaneous tissue; leathery, dry, does not blanch, painless): takes **more than 8 weeks** and requires surgery.
- Source for all four rows: [StatPearls, Burn Classification (Warby & Maani; updated 26 Sep 2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)

**Chronic wounds: percentage area reduction (PAR) as a predictor**
- WBP 2021: "If a wound is not at least 20–40% smaller by week 4, it is unlikely to heal by week 12." — [Sibbald et al. 2021/2022](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/wcetcn/volume-42-number-1/wound-bed-preparation)
- Diabetic foot ulcers:
  - **≥53% reduction over the first 4 weeks** was a robust predictor of complete healing at 12 weeks (Sheehan et al., Diabetes Care 2003;26(6):1879–82).
  - **≥50% reduction by week 4** (Snyder et al., Ostomy Wound Manage 2010;56(3):44–50; n=250 from two RCTs): sensitivity 95%, specificity 62%, PPV 54%, NPV 96%. Week-4 diagnostic efficiency (AUC) was 0.91, versus 0.71 at week 1, 0.82 at week 2 and 0.86 at week 3.
  - Source for both: [Evidence Corner, WOUNDS (HMP Global), secondary summary](https://www.hmpgloballearningnetwork.com/site/wounds/content/chronic-wounds-and-delayed-healing-risk)
  - The summary page's wording of Snyder's NPV appears garbled; verify against the original. PubMed abstract (not fetched): [Snyder 2010 post-hoc analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20368673/)
- Venous leg ulcers:
  - **≥40% reduction in the first 3 weeks** predicts healing (Phillips et al., J Am Acad Dermatol 2000;43(4):627–30).
  - **≥30% reduction in the first 2 weeks** predicts healing for properly compressed ulcers (van Rijswijk 1993, J Fam Pract).
  - Source for both: [Evidence Corner, WOUNDS](https://www.hmpgloballearningnetwork.com/site/wounds/content/chronic-wounds-and-delayed-healing-risk)
- Deep pressure ulcers: **≥39% reduction in 2 weeks** (van Rijswijk & Polansky 1994, WOUNDS). — [Evidence Corner, WOUNDS](https://www.hmpgloballearningnetwork.com/site/wounds/content/chronic-wounds-and-delayed-healing-risk)
- Study guide benchmark: a 20–40% area reduction after 2–4 weeks; failure to reach it signals a recalcitrant wound. — [Open Exam Prep (low-quality source)](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/abwm-cwca/assessment-and-diagnosis/measure-framework-ryb)

**Real-world durations (chronic, for context only)**
- In a UK tissue-viability audit of heel ulcers, mean treatment duration was **51.9 days** and mean time to complete debridement (black/yellow to red) was **24.3 days**. These are audit results, not standards. — [Gray et al., Wounds UK 2006](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)
- Hypergranulation pathway: after treating infected friable tissue, reassess after **six weeks**. — [Oxford Health NHS Hypergranulation Pathway 2023](https://oxfordhealth.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/51/2024/01/Hypergranulation-Pathway-FINAL.pdf)

### Inferences
Plain-language timeline a lay guide could use, synthesised from the sources above (not a validated clinical tool):
- **Day 0–3/5 (inflammation):** the area around the wound may be slightly red, warm and swollen. This is normal and should **settle, not spread**.
- **From about day 3–7:** in a clean wound, red granulation (deeper wounds) or pink new skin from the edges (shallow wounds) should start to appear.
- **Shallow wounds** (lecet, superficial burns): roughly 1–3 weeks to close (by analogy with burn data: 5–10 days if epidermis only, 2–3 weeks if superficial dermis).
- **Most acute wounds:** expected to close within about **4–6 weeks**. A wound open longer than this is "chronic" by the StatPearls definition and needs professional review.
- **Practical home check:** if a wound is not clearly smaller after 2 weeks, or not at least about one-third smaller by 4 weeks, see a health worker. This adapts the chronic-wound PAR rules (20–40% at 4 weeks; 30–40% at 2–3 weeks) to lay language.
- **Scars** keep remodelling for months up to 1–2 years and never regain more than about 80% of normal skin strength.
- **Black or yellow wounds take longer** because dead tissue must be removed before granulation can complete. The audit's mean debridement time of about 24 days for heel ulcers shows the scale, but that was a chronic population.

### Gaps
- **Epithelialisation rate in mm/day from the wound edges:** no reliable source was retrieved (searches returned only lab/model papers). Do not quote a number without verification.
- **Granulation appearing from about day 3–5:** StatPearls says days 5–7 for fibroblast activity; Arisanty frames proliferation as starting from day 2. No fetched source states "granulation visible from day 3–5" for acute wounds.
- **Gelfand 2002, Flanagan 2003 and Cardinal 2008 PAR thresholds** were not retrieved. The commonly quoted "40% in 4 weeks for VLU" was not confirmed; the retrieved VLU figure is 40% in 3 weeks (Phillips 2000).
- **No evidence found on time spent in each color for minor acute wounds** (abrasions, lacerations, bites). This is a real evidence gap in the literature, not just in this search.

## Q4. What color changes signal a problem, and how fast should a lay user seek care?

### Takeaway
These warning signs are relevant to a public guide:
- **Granulation changes:** pale ("merah pucat") or dark/dusky granulation; bright red, friable granulation that bleeds easily; hypergranulation rising above skin level.
- **New dead tissue:** new or increasing yellow/black tissue.
- **Spreading signs:** spreading redness/warmth/swelling, pus, increasing smell or pain, wound enlargement.
- **Systemic signs:** feeling unwell.

The IWII Wound Infection Continuum grades these from subtle local signs, to spreading, to systemic. Spreading or systemic signs warrant same-day or urgent care.

### Cited Findings
**IWII wound infection continuum (2016 revision)**
- The stages are: contamination, then colonisation, then local infection (covert/subtle and overt/classic), then spreading infection, then systemic infection. "Critical colonisation" was removed. — [Haesler E, Ousey K. "Evolution of the wound infection continuum", Wounds International 2018;9(4) (reporting the 2016 IWII revision)](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)
- **Covert (subtle) signs of local infection:**
  - hypergranulation (excessive "vascular" tissue)
  - **bleeding, friable granulation**
  - epithelial bridging and pocketing
  - wound breakdown and enlargement
  - delayed healing beyond expectations
  - new or increasing pain
  - increasing malodour
  - [Haesler & Ousey 2018](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)
- **Overt (classic) signs:** erythema, local warmth, swelling, purulent discharge, wound breakdown/dehiscence with or without satellite lesions, increasing malodour and pain, delayed healing. — [Haesler & Ousey 2018](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)
- **Spreading infection:** extending erythema or induration, crepitus, lymphangitis, inflamed lymph glands, malaise, lethargy, loss of appetite. **Systemic:** severe sepsis, septic shock, organ failure, death. — [Haesler & Ousey 2018](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/68b2c14eb4b474454e0439f8cb6cb0b0.pdf)

**Sibbald WBP 2021: NERDS and STONEES**
- **NERDS** (local/superficial infection, any 3 or more): **N**onhealing, **E**xudate increase, **R**ed friable granulation, **D**ebris/dead tissue, **S**mell. — [Sibbald et al. 2021/2022](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/wcetcn/volume-42-number-1/wound-bed-preparation)
- **STONEES** (deep/surrounding infection, any 3 or more): increased **S**ize; elevated **T**emperature (about 3°F above mirror-image skin); probes to b**O**ne; **N**ew satellite areas; **E**xudate; **E**rythema/**E**dema (cellulitis); **S**mell. Erythema is hard to see in darker skin or when oedema is present. — [Sibbald et al. 2021/2022](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/wcetcn/volume-42-number-1/wound-bed-preparation)

**Hypergranulation (Oxford Community Tissue Viability Service, 2023)**
- **Definition and appearance:** "excessive granulation that protrudes above the wound surface". It blocks new epidermis from migrating across the wound, so epithelialisation stalls. It is pink to red, moist, shiny, soft/spongy, bleeds easily and is usually painless. In infection it can be red to dark brown and friable.
- **Causes:** infection/biofilm, excess moisture or occlusion, friction, inflammation/foreign material, stress.
- **Escalation:** refer urgently (dermatology) if the tissue is irregular, hard, cauliflower-like, extends beyond the wound margins, persists for months or does not respond to treatment, because these signs may indicate malignancy.
- Source: [Oxford Health NHS, Hypergranulation Pathway v3 (2023)](https://oxfordhealth.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/51/2024/01/Hypergranulation-Pathway-FINAL.pdf)

**Other color warning signs**
- Pale red ("merah pucat") granulation may indicate biofilm. — [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)
- In light skin, erythema "may appear worse than it is", which can lead to over-prescribing of antibiotics. For suspected cellulitis, "the skin will feel tighter when touched". — [Wounds International 2023, Wound Care and Skin Tone](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/MULTI23_ME_SkinTones_WINT-web.pdf)

### Inferences
Suggested urgency tiers for a lay guide. These are the researcher's synthesis from IWII/WBP; the sources themselves do not give lay timeframes.
- **Seek EMERGENCY care now (IGD/UGD):**
  - feeling generally unwell, fever/chills, confusion
  - redness, swelling or hardness spreading quickly from the wound, or red streaks up the limb (lymphangitis)
  - crackling under the skin (crepitus)
  - black/grey skin spreading around the wound
  - severe pain out of proportion to the wound
  - These map to IWII "spreading/systemic" signs.
- **See a health worker within 24 hours (Puskesmas/klinik):**
  - pus (nanah) (yellow, green or other color)
  - new foul smell
  - increasing redness/warmth/swelling around the wound
  - wound getting bigger or reopening
  - increasing pain after the first 2–3 days
  - These map to IWII "overt local infection".
- **Book a check-up within a few days:**
  - granulation that bleeds very easily or is dark/dull red or pale
  - red tissue rising above skin level
  - yellow layer that keeps coming back
  - new black area
  - These map to IWII "covert" signs and NERDS.
- **Not healing:** no clear improvement after about 2 weeks, or still open at 4–6 weeks.
- People with diabetes, poor circulation, weak immunity or older age should use lower thresholds. This general clinical principle is consistent with the StatPearls list of delaying factors (diabetes, smoking, malnutrition, ischaemia, steroids). — [StatPearls, Wound Healing Phases (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470443/)

### Gaps
- **Green/blue-green exudate as a sign of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*:** the fetched IWII continuum article contains no statement on it, and no source was retrieved in this session. It is widely taught but should be verified, for example in IWII 2022 *Wound Infection in Clinical Practice*.
- **IWII 2022 consensus document:** not fetched directly; the 2016 continuum (via Haesler & Ousey 2018) was used. Check whether the 2022 edition changed the covert/overt sign lists.
- No Indonesian (Kemenkes) public guidance on when to seek care for an infected wound was retrieved.
- **Dark red/dusky granulation as a sign of ischaemia or infection:** not explicitly sourced beyond "red to dark brown, friable" infected hypergranulation (Oxford 2023) and "red friable granulation" (NERDS).

## Q5. What are the limitations of color-based assessment, especially on darker (brown) skin?

### Takeaway
Color is a simplification:
- It ignores depth, infection, exudate and the wound's cause.
- Mixed-color wounds are the norm.
- Yellow is defined inconsistently across systems.
- Black or yellow tissue hides how deep the wound is.

On brown and dark skin, redness (erythema), a key sign of inflammation and infection, is often not seen as "red". In one multicentre study, clinicians recorded erythema about six times less often in dark skin (2.3% vs 13.4%) and almost completely missed heavy bacterial loads. This matters for Indonesian readers: the guide should teach warmth, swelling, hardness, pain, smell and comparison with the other side of the body, not redness alone.

### Cited Findings
**General limitations of color coding**
- Color-only coding is "an over simplification", because infection and exudate also need assessment. — [Gray et al., Wounds UK 2006 (citing White 2003)](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)
- Wounds with ≥50% yellow/black necrosis are "unstageable": color hides depth. Necrosis is not automatically infection. — [Poltekkes Malang KTI citing Arisanty 2013](https://perpus-utama.poltekkes-malang.ac.id/assets/file/kti/1501460008/BAB_2.pdf)
- The Dutch WCS RYB counts infection signs within "yellow". Other systems put infection on a separate continuum. Color categories are not standardised internationally. — [Nictiz zib Wound v3.0 (2016)](https://zibs.nl/wiki/Wound-v3.0(2016EN)); [Gray et al. 2006](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)
- WCS color classification is not suitable for oncological (malignant) wounds. — [Nictiz zib Wound v3.0 (2016)](https://zibs.nl/wiki/Wound-v3.0(2016EN))
- Mixed wounds are assessed by recording tissue types as percentages totalling 100% and treating the "worst" color first. — [Open Exam Prep (low-quality source)](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/abwm-cwca/assessment-and-diagnosis/measure-framework-ryb); [Gray et al. 2006, "tissue furthest to the left"](https://wounds-uk.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/content_9064.pdf)
- Hypergranulation that looks like healthy red tissue can actually be a barrier to healing, and in rare cases a malignancy. — [Oxford Health NHS 2023](https://oxfordhealth.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/51/2024/01/Hypergranulation-Pathway-FINAL.pdf)

**Skin tone (most relevant for Indonesian readers)**
- **Johnson J, et al. "Skin Pigmentation Impacts the Clinical Diagnosis of Wound Infection: Imaging of Bacterial Burden to Overcome Diagnostic Limitations." J Racial Ethn Health Disparities 2024;11:1045–1055 (online 11 Apr 2023).** Post hoc analysis of the FLAAG trial: 350 chronic wounds, 14 US centres, 20 clinicians; patients grouped by Fitzpatrick skin type.
  - **Erythema recorded:** 13.4% (types I–II), 7.2% (III–IV), **2.3% (V–VI)**; p=0.05, described as "near significant".
  - **Bacterial loads:** did not differ by skin type (median 1.8×10⁶ CFU/g).
  - **Sensitivity of clinical signs for high bacterial load (>10⁴ CFU/g):** 14.0%, 24.3% and **2.9%** respectively. That is 4.8- to 8.4-fold lower in the darkest group (p=0.003 vs light; p=0.04 vs medium).
  - Quote: "Clinicians were less likely to flag bacterial-laden wounds on highly pigmented skin as being problematic."
  - Source: [Springer, open access](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40615-023-01584-8)
  - Caveat: chronic wounds, US population. The outcome is bacterial load, not confirmed clinical infection.
- **Wounds International 2023 skin tone consensus (Dhoonmoon, Nair, Abbas et al.):**
  - "erythema does not appear as redness in many skin tones". Check "swelling, warmth and pain".
  - "Use all of the senses, especially touch". Warmth can be felt or measured with an infrared thermometer. Ask whether skin "feels spongy or firm" and "looks or feels shiny or tight".
  - Compare with other body areas ("It can be easier to spot when affected areas are compared with other parts of the body").
  - Use good, natural lighting.
  - "Ask the patient about their skin and listen to their perspective."
  - Severe diabetic foot ulcers in dark skin may show black or brown eschar.
  - Source: [Wounds International 2023](https://woundsinternational.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/MULTI23_ME_SkinTones_WINT-web.pdf)
- WBP 2021 also notes erythema is difficult to recognise in darker skin or when oedema is present. Its STONEES "temperature" criterion (about 3°F warmer than mirror-image skin) is an alternative to color. — [Sibbald et al. 2021/2022](https://journals.cambridgemedia.com.au/wcetcn/volume-42-number-1/wound-bed-preparation)

### Inferences
- **Two different color questions:**
  - The **wound bed** colors (red granulation, yellow slough, black eschar, pink epithelium) are tissue colors and look broadly similar across skin tones.
  - The **surrounding skin** color (redness = inflammation/infection) is where brown skin causes problems. Redness may show as darker brown, purple-ish, or not show at all.
  - The guide should say this explicitly.
- **New epithelium and scars on brown skin:** new pink epithelium may look pinker or lighter than the surrounding brown skin and later darken (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation). This is expected, not a warning sign. This is researcher inference; no fetched source addressed it.
- **Lay checklist for brown skin ("kulit sawo matang"):**
  - Feel for warmth with the back of the hand, comparing with the same spot on the other side of the body.
  - Feel for swelling or hardness.
  - Notice increasing pain, smell or pus.
  - Notice whether the skin looks shiny or tight.
  - Check whether the darker area is spreading. Marking its edge with a pen and re-checking in 12–24 hours is a common clinical practice, not sourced here.
- **Lighting and photos:** look at the wound in daylight. Phone photos taken in the same light each day help track change. The 2023 consensus recommends natural light and a calibrated color chart for clinical photography.

### Gaps
- No study found on color-based wound assessment specifically in Southeast Asian / Indonesian (Fitzpatrick III–V) populations.
- No source fetched on how wound-bed tissue colors (as opposed to periwound erythema) differ across skin tones.
- The Wounds International 2023 consensus gave no numbers on delayed diagnosis in wound care. The only quantitative skin-tone data retrieved is Johnson et al. 2023/2024.
- No validated lay (non-clinician) color-assessment tool was found. All systems above were designed for clinicians.
