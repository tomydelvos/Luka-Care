## 1. Burn depth classes: skin layer, color/appearance, bedside signs, healing time, scarring, home vs medical care

### Takeaway
Depth runs on a continuum, and sources draw the lines slightly differently. Their bedside picture is still consistent. Superficial burns are red, dry, painful, blanch, have no blisters and heal in about 3–10 days. Superficial partial-thickness burns are pink or red, moist and blistered, very painful, blanch with brisk refill, and heal in about 2–3 weeks (≤14 days in the ANZBA "superficial dermal" category). Deep partial-thickness burns are mottled (pink-white or blotchy dark red) and drier, with sluggish or absent blanching and reduced pain; they take more than 3 weeks (3–8 weeks) and scar. Full-thickness burns are white, waxy or leathery, dry, non-blanching and painless; they need surgery or grafting, and without it take more than 8 weeks and heal by contracture. Fourth-degree burns are black or charred, involve muscle or bone, do not heal on their own and often need amputation.

### Cited Findings
- StatPearls describes the superficial (first-degree) burn as epidermis-only, pink to red, dry, without blisters and moderately painful. It heals in 5–10 days without scarring. — [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)
- StatPearls describes the superficial partial-thickness burn as involving the superficial dermis. The wound bed is homogeneously red or pink once the blister is unroofed, it blanches with pressure, it is painful, and it heals in 2–3 weeks with minimal scarring. — [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)
- StatPearls describes the deep partial-thickness burn as involving the reticular dermis. The wound bed is mottled, blanches sluggishly, and pain is minimal or felt only with deep pressure. Healing takes longer than superficial partial-thickness; it "can heal without surgery," but scarring is "unavoidable." — [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)
- StatPearls describes the full-thickness burn as leathery, stiff, dry, non-blanching and painless because nerves are destroyed. It takes more than 8 weeks and "requires surgical treatment." The page does not cover fourth-degree burns. — [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)
- AAFP 2020: superficial burns are painful, erythematous, dry, blanching and unblistered, and heal in 3–6 days. Superficial partial-thickness burns are exudative, blanch, may blister and mostly heal within 3 weeks. Deep partial-thickness burns are drier, do not blanch and take more than 3 weeks. Full-thickness burns are white, waxy, dry and leathery with reduced sensation, take 3 weeks or more and usually need surgery and grafting. — [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html)
- AAFP 2020 lists hypertrophic scarring and hyperpigmentation as long-term complications of partial-thickness burns, and recommends sun protection after healing. — [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html)
- Indonesia's PNPK (2019) does not use derajat I/IIA/IIB/III/IV as its main depth scheme. It uses the five ANZBA categories: Epidermal (merah, no bula, cap refill present, sensation present, heals in about 7 days without a mark), Superficial dermal (merah muda pucat, small bula, cap refill present, painful, heals within 14 days with a colour difference), Mid dermal (merah muda gelap, bula present, slow cap refill, +/- sensation, usually but not always heals spontaneously), Deep dermal (merah tua bercak, +/- bula, no cap refill, no sensation, does not heal or heals slowly with eschar) and Full thickness (putih, no bula, no cap refill, no sensation, does not heal, thick eschar). The terms "derajat 2A, 2B, 3" appear only in its dressing and surgery sections. — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf)
- WikEM's chart says deep partial-thickness burns are pale white-yellow with absent capillary refill and heal in 3–8 weeks; they are likely to scar if healing takes more than 3 weeks, and a skin graft may be needed if the burn is not healed within 3 weeks. Full-thickness burns heal in more than 8 weeks by contracture and almost always need grafting. Fourth-degree burns (fat, muscle, bone) are black and charred with eschar, do not heal and frequently need amputation. — [WK](https://wikem.org/wiki/Template:Burn_thickness_chart)
- **Disagreement on healing time, superficial (first-degree):** 3–6 days [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html), about 7 days [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf), 5–10 days [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/) and [WK](https://wikem.org/wiki/Template:Burn_thickness_chart).
- **Disagreement on healing time, superficial partial-thickness:** within 14 days for "superficial dermal" [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf); 2–3 weeks [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/); within or under 3 weeks [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html), [WK](https://wikem.org/wiki/Template:Burn_thickness_chart).
- **Disagreement on blanching, deep partial-thickness:** "blanches sluggishly" [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/); "does not blanch" [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html); absent capillary refill [WK](https://wikem.org/wiki/Template:Burn_thickness_chart). The ANZBA scheme in [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf) separates "mid dermal" (slow refill) from "deep dermal" (absent refill), which partly explains the conflict.
- **Disagreement on color, deep partial-thickness:** "mottled" [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/); "pale white-yellow" [WK](https://wikem.org/wiki/Template:Burn_thickness_chart); "merah tua bercak" (blotchy dark red) [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf).
- **Disagreement on scarring, superficial partial-thickness:** "minimal" [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/); "heals without scarring" [WK](https://wikem.org/wiki/Template:Burn_thickness_chart); can cause long-term hypertrophic scarring and hyperpigmentation [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html); heals "dengan perbedaan warna" (with a colour difference) [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf).
- Superficial (first-degree) burns are not counted in %TBSA. Superficial partial-thickness, deep partial-thickness and full-thickness burns are counted. — [ABA](https://ameriburn.org/burnreferral); [SP](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)
- On who needs care: the ABA recommends burn-centre consultation for "all potentially deep burns of any size" and for partial-thickness burns under 10% TBSA. It recommends immediate consultation with consideration for transfer for any full-thickness burn. — [ABA](https://ameriburn.org/burnreferral)

### Inferences
- Rough crosswalk between the schemes (my own mapping, not stated as such in the sources): ANZBA "Epidermal" ≈ superficial/first-degree; "Superficial dermal" ≈ superficial partial-thickness (Indonesian clinicians' "derajat IIA"); "Deep dermal" ≈ deep partial-thickness ("derajat IIB"); "Full thickness" ≈ third-degree ("derajat III"). "Mid dermal" sits on the border, and its behaviour is unpredictable.
- Practical rules for lay readers that follow from the sources:
  - **Treat at home:** red, dry, no blisters, painful, blanches (epidermal/first-degree), and small.
  - **Usually treatable at home or a puskesmas if small and not in a special area:** blisters on a pink, wet, very painful base that blanches (superficial partial-thickness). It should heal within about 2–3 weeks.
  - **Needs medical care:** a mottled, dark-red-blotchy, pale/white or dry base; slow or absent blanching; reduced pain. These are possible deep burns, and the ABA says any potentially deep burn of any size should get consultation.
  - **Hospital or burn unit:** white/waxy/leathery, brown/black or charred, painless (full-thickness or fourth-degree).
- Healing time works as a self-check for lay readers. A burn that has not healed by about 2–3 weeks was probably deeper than it looked, and the reader should seek care. This rests on the [WK](https://wikem.org/wiki/Template:Burn_thickness_chart) "graft may be needed if not healed within 3 weeks" statement and the AAFP depth definitions.
- "Less pain" is a warning sign, not a reassurance. Every source links reduced or absent pain to deeper burns.

### Gaps
- Capillary refill is not specified in StatPearls for any category. Only PNPK/ANZBA and WikEM state refill explicitly.
- Fourth-degree data come only from a wiki (WikEM). StatPearls "Burn Classification" does not cover it. I could not open Jeschke et al., *Nat Rev Dis Primers* 2020 ("Burn injury") or the ANZBA EMSB manual directly; a fetch was blocked.
- The widely cited link between healing time and hypertrophic scarring (Deitch 1983; Cubison 2006: much higher hypertrophic scar risk when healing takes more than 21 days, especially in children) could not be verified. The full texts (Burns & Trauma 2016 s41038-016-0068-2; PMC11958538) were blocked, so no percentages are given here.
- Figures on how accurate clinical depth assessment is (often quoted as roughly 60–75% for experienced clinicians, with laser Doppler imaging as the better tool) could not be verified in this session.
- I did not reach the ISBI Practice Guidelines (2016/2018) to cross-check the depth definitions.

## 2. How burn color and depth change over the first 24–72 hours (burn wound conversion) and why depth is reassessed

### Takeaway
A burn that looks superficial at first can deepen ("convert") over the first 1–3 days. The most damaging window is about 12–24 hours, and conversion can continue for several days. AAFP advises reassessing minor-looking burns at 24–72 hours. Depth judged at first aid is provisional; the reliable "final" depth is clearer at around 48–72 hours, once blisters are removed.

### Cited Findings
- "Minor-appearing burns" can worsen over time (burn wound conversion) and need reassessment "in 24 to 72 hours." Superficial partial-thickness burns can deepen to deep partial-thickness or full-thickness "within 48 hours." Depth can also increase with inadequate treatment or superinfection. — [AAFP 2020](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html)
- Burn wound deepening is defined as "the conversion of superficial burns to deep burns." Necrosis spreads into the initially viable **zone of stasis**, which surrounds the **zone of coagulation** (immediate necrosis). — [LU 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9634408)
- Damage in the zone of stasis progresses "especially in the 12–24 h after the injury," and secondary damage may spread "in the first few days." — [LU 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9634408)
- Drivers of deepening named in the review include:
  - wound infection ("one of the important factors leading to early burn wound deepening")
  - microthrombosis and a hypercoagulable state that worsen microcirculation
  - neutrophil-driven oxygen free radicals and proteolytic enzymes
  - improper early treatment that "makes the wound become the source of infection"

  The review also recommends keeping blister skin to maintain a moist environment. — [LU 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9634408)
- The same review cites an experimental study in which moderate hypothermia (31–33 °C for 4 h, starting 2 h after the burn) gave "a 23% reduction in burn depth" at 24 h versus controls. It also warns that over-cooling causes vasoconstriction that can worsen the wound. — [LU 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9634408)
- StatPearls notes that blisters "may be intact at first evaluation" in both superficial and deep partial-thickness burns. The wound-bed color (homogeneous pink vs mottled) is assessed "once the blister is unroofed / after blisters are debrided," so intact blisters hide the true color at first. — [SP 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)
- Adequate first aid was associated with lower odds of a burn being **full-thickness** (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2–0.6). This fits the idea that early cooling limits progression. — [GRIFFIN 2020](https://em-consulte.com/article/1340436/cool-running-water-first-aid-decreases-skin-grafti)

### Inferences
- In lay terms: on day 0 a burn may look pink, blistered and painful, but by days 2–3 it may look mottled, pale or white, or less painful. That change means it has become deeper and needs medical review. A guidebook should tell readers to look at the burn again on day 2–3 rather than judge it only on the day of injury.
- What makes conversion more likely and is relevant to lay first aid: no or short cooling, ice (over-cooling and vasoconstriction), infection, letting the wound dry out, and home remedies that contaminate the wound (an inference from LU's "improper early treatment" and NHS advice against oils and butter).

### Gaps
- I found no source that describes the visible color sequence of conversion hour by hour (for example, pink and blanching to mottled to white and non-blanching). The color-change description above is inferred from the depth definitions plus the AAFP and LU timing statements.
- LU 2022 does not mention the 48–72 h figure or Jackson's third zone (hyperaemia). The 24–72 h reassessment window comes from AAFP. The specific "reassess depth at 48–72 h" recommendation in ISBI 2016 and ANZBA EMSB was not verified directly.
- LU's summary does not make clear whether the 23% depth-reduction result came from an animal or human study; it is most likely an animal model.

## 3. First aid evidence: cool running water for 20 minutes within 3 hours

### Takeaway
Guidelines now agree on cool running tap water for 20 minutes, started as soon as possible and still worthwhile up to 3 hours after the burn. Use no ice and no butter, oils or creams. In a cohort of 2,495 children, this "adequate first aid" was associated with about 40% lower odds of skin grafting and 60% lower odds of a full-thickness burn. Indonesia's 2019 PNPK does not state a cooling duration, which leaves a gap for a local guidebook to fill from international guidance.

### Cited Findings
- Griffin et al. (2020) studied 2,495 children with burns, using a prospective registry at a tertiary paediatric hospital in Australia. "Adequate first aid" was defined as "20 minutes of cool running water within 3 hours of injury." Results:
  - 90.6% of children received running-water first aid; 71.3% received the adequate duration.
  - Overall, 9.5% needed grafting.
  - With adequate first aid, the odds ratios were: grafting 0.6 (95% CI 0.4–0.8); full-thickness depth 0.4 (0.2–0.6); hospital admission 0.7 (0.3–0.9); operating-room interventions 0.7 (0.5–0.9).
  - Length of stay was not significantly different (HR 0.9, 0.7–1.2; P=.48).
  - The abstract did not report a re-epithelialization (healing time) result.

  — [GRIFFIN 2020](https://em-consulte.com/article/1340436/cool-running-water-first-aid-decreases-skin-grafti)
- AAFP 2020: cool with running tap water at "46.4°F [8°C] to 77°F [25°C]" for "at least 20 minutes." Start within 30 minutes; benefit may still occur up to 3 hours later. Wet dressings do not cool effectively, and ice should not be used. — [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html)
- NHS (reviewed 2026): hold the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes, as soon as possible and within 3 hours. If there is no running water, use cool bottled water or a wet towel. Remove clothing and jewellery near the burn but leave anything stuck to it. Afterwards, cover loosely with cling film without wrapping it around. Do not use creams, oils, butter, plasters or sticky dressings, and do not burst blisters. — [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/burns-and-scalds/)
- Indonesian PNPK 2019 gives no cooling duration, water temperature or time window. It covers responder PPE, keeping the patient warm, and tap water as safe and effective for wound cleaning and irrigation. — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf)
- Over-cooling (supercooling) can cause vasoconstriction and worsen the wound. — [LU 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9634408)

### Inferences
- For Indonesian readers, the clearest message is: *"Aliri air keran yang sejuk (bukan es, bukan air es) selama 20 menit, secepatnya — masih bermanfaat sampai 3 jam setelah kejadian. Jangan oles pasta gigi, mentega, minyak, kecap, atau ramuan."* ("Run cool tap water over it — not ice or iced water — for 20 minutes, as soon as possible; it still helps up to 3 hours afterwards. Do not apply toothpaste, butter, oil, soy sauce or herbal remedies.") Toothpaste and kecap are common local folk remedies. None of the sources mention toothpaste specifically; NHS rules out creams, oils and butter.
- Keep the person, especially a child, warm while cooling the burned area. The PNPK says to keep the patient warm, and LU warns against over-cooling.
- The Griffin data support a claim that proper cooling can reduce the need for skin grafting by about 40% (OR 0.6). Phrase it as an association from an observational study, not a guarantee.

### Gaps
- I could not access the Cuttle et al. / Rajan et al. porcine studies (Burns, about 2008–2010), which are the origin of the "20 minutes" and "up to 3 hours" thresholds. The ISBI 2016 Part 1 first-aid recommendation was also not verified directly.
- The Griffin abstract did not report healing time (re-epithelialization) or ICU outcomes.
- No Indonesian data on first-aid practices (for example, how often toothpaste or ice is used) were found in this session.

## 4. Size estimation for lay people (palm rule) and when to seek medical or burn-unit care

### Takeaway
The patient's own palm including the fingers is about 1% TBSA (the palm alone is about 0.5%). This is best for small or scattered burns; first-degree redness is not counted. Referral thresholds differ by guideline. ANZBA (adopted in Indonesia's PNPK) uses more than 10% TBSA in adults, more than 5% in children, and more than 5% full-thickness. The ABA uses partial-thickness of 10% or more, and any full-thickness burn. All guidelines agree on special areas (face, hands, feet, genitals, perineum, major joints), circumferential burns, chemical, electrical and inhalation injuries, and the very young or old.

### Cited Findings
- **Palm rule:** the palm including fingers is about 1% TBSA, and without fingers about 0.5%. The palm method may overestimate area in large burns. — [AAFP 2020](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html)
- The ABA uses the "Palmar Method" (patient's palm ≈ 1% TBSA) for small or scattered burns, generally under 15–20% TBSA. It recommends the Rule of Nines for adults and the Lund and Browder chart for children. — [ABA](https://ameriburn.org/burnreferral)
- PNPK (Indonesia) gives the palm including fingers as about 1% TBSA, for small, scattered burns. It describes the adult Rule of Nines (perineum 1%), noting it is not accurate for children. For the paediatric adjustment, the head share falls by 1% per year after 12 months and each leg rises by 0.5% per year; a child is considered proportional to an adult after age 10. — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf)
- **ANZBA referral criteria, reproduced in PNPK Tabel 6 ("Kriteria rujuk"):**
  1. Burns over 10% TBSA
  2. Burns over 5% TBSA in children
  3. Full-thickness burns over 5% TBSA
  4. Special areas: face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum, major joints, circumferential burns of a limb or the chest
  5. Inhalation injury
  6. Electrical burns
  7. Chemical burns
  8. Comorbidities
  9. Major trauma
  10. Extremes of age
  11. Pregnancy
  12. Non-accidental burns

  — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf)
- **PNPK severity classes:**
  - *Ringan* (mild): adults ≤15% TBSA; children ≤10%; full-thickness ≤2% not involving eyes, ears, face, hands, feet or perineum.
  - *Sedang* (moderate): adults 15–25% with full-thickness under 10%; partial-thickness 10–20% in children under 10 or adults over 40; full-thickness ≤10% without cosmetic or special-area issues.
  - *Berat* (severe): ≥25% TBSA; ≥20% in children under 10 or adults over 40; full-thickness ≥10%; eyes, face, ears, hands, feet or perineum with functional or cosmetic impairment; all electrical burns; all burns with major trauma or inhalation injury; all patients in poor condition.

  — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf)
- **ABA, current web guideline (page copyright 2025):**
  - *Immediate consultation with consideration for transfer:* full-thickness burns; partial-thickness burns ≥10% TBSA; any deep partial or full-thickness burn of the face, hands, genitalia, feet or perineum, or over any joint; burns with comorbidities; concomitant trauma; poorly controlled pain; all suspected inhalation injuries; all chemical injuries; high-voltage (≥1,000 V) and lightning injuries.
  - *Children (≤14 years or <30 kg):* "all pediatric burns may benefit" from burn-centre referral, because of pain, dressing changes, rehabilitation, caregiver needs or possible non-accidental trauma.
  - *Consultation recommended:* partial-thickness burns under 10%; "all potentially deep burns of any size"; possible inhalation signs (facial flash burns, singed facial hair, smoke exposure); low-voltage (<1,000 V) electrical injuries.

  — [ABA](https://ameriburn.org/burnreferral)
- **ABA criteria, older version as tabulated in AAFP 2020:** burns of the face, hands, feet, major joints, genitalia or perineum; children in facilities without paediatric capability; concurrent trauma; electrical or chemical burns; full-thickness burns at any age; over 10% TBSA; inhalation injury; need for special support; pre-existing medical conditions. — [AAFP 2020](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html); StatPearls also cites the older ABA list with TBSA over 10% — [SP 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539773/)
- **NHS (UK, for the public):**
  - Call 999 or go to A&E if the burn is very large or deep; is on the face, genitals or bottom; or was caused by acid, a chemical or electricity. Do not drive yourself.
  - Call NHS 111 if unsure, and for any child under 5.

  — [NHS 2026](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/burns-and-scalds/)
- **Where the thresholds disagree:**
  - Adults: ANZBA/PNPK refer at over 10% TBSA; the ABA calls for immediate consultation at ≥10% partial-thickness.
  - Children: ANZBA/PNPK refer at over 5%; the ABA now suggests all paediatric burns may benefit from referral.
  - Full-thickness: ANZBA/PNPK refer at over 5% full-thickness; the ABA refers for any full-thickness burn.
  - The PNPK's own "ringan" class allows up to 15% (adult) or 10% (child), which is looser than its own ANZBA referral table.

  — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf); [ABA](https://ameriburn.org/burnreferral)

### Inferences
- Lay-friendly rule for the guidebook (my synthesis of the sources):
  - **Go to a doctor or IGD** if a blistered or deeper burn is larger than about 1 palm (about 1% TBSA) on a child or larger than 2–3 palms on an adult.
  - **Go to a doctor or IGD** for any burn that looks white, grey, brown, black, leathery or painless.
  - **Go to a doctor or IGD** for any burn on the face, hands, feet, genitals, buttocks or perineum, or over a major joint; for any burn that goes all the way around a limb, finger or the chest; and for any chemical, electrical or smoke/fire-in-an-enclosed-space injury.
  - **Go to a doctor or IGD** for a baby, toddler, older person, pregnant woman or someone with a chronic illness.
  - **Go to a doctor or IGD** for any burn not healing in about 2 weeks.

  The 1-palm threshold for children is more conservative than any cited threshold; it is a pragmatic lay simplification, and NHS similarly advises calling 111 for any child under 5.
- Lay readers should not count simple red skin without blisters (first-degree) toward size, matching ABA and StatPearls guidance.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the downloadable full ABA PDF; the web summary did not list circumferential burns or define "major joints." ANZBA EMSB (current edition) and ISBI referral criteria were not verified directly; ANZBA's criteria are taken from the PNPK reproduction.
- The NHS page does not give a numeric size threshold or mention hands, feet or white/charred appearance; an older "larger than the person's hand" NHS wording could not be confirmed on the current page.

## 5. Common burns in Indonesia / Southeast Asia (scalds, hot oil, motorcycle exhaust) and Indonesian guidance

### Takeaway
The main Indonesian clinical guidance is the PNPK Tata Laksana Luka Bakar (KMK HK.01.07/MENKES/555/2019), which follows ANZBA EMSB. Its hospital data show hot water (scald) as the leading cause in children (52%) and flame in adults (53.1%), with contact burns at 15% in children. Motorcycle exhaust (knalpot) contact burns can be deep: in an Australian paediatric series, every admitted child needed skin grafting. I found no peer-reviewed Indonesian study on knalpot burns.

### Cited Findings
- PNPK 2019 data from RSCM (Jakarta, 2012–2016), by cause:
  - Adults: flame ("api") 53.1%, hot water ("air panas") 19.1%, electrical 14%, contact 5%, chemical 3%.
  - Children: hot water 52%, flame 26%, contact 15%, electrical 6%, chemical 1%.

  — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf)
- PNPK 2019 hospital figures: RSCM 2011–2012 had 303 patients, male:female 2.26:1, mean age 25.7 years. RSCM mortality was 34% (2012) and 33% (2011). RSU Sanglah had 13 deaths among 154 patients (8.42%); RSUD Soetomo had 15 deaths among 145 (10.3%). National data were not available. The document does not mention cooking oil ("minyak") or exhaust ("knalpot") as causes. — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf)
- Motorcycle exhaust burns in children (NSW, Australia, 2008–2011): 96 children aged 16 or under were referred to the Children's Hospital at Westmead (mean age 8.6 years; 78% male). 41 (43%) were admitted, and "all required skin grafting." Mean length of stay was 2.4 days, and 7% had wound infections. Settings were mostly backyards (47%) and farms (17%). In an illustrative case (a 13-year-old's leg), laser Doppler imaging showed "a deep-dermal/full-thickness burn centrally." The letter does not explain why these burns are deep and gives no exhaust temperatures. — [MJA 2013](https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2013/198/10/burns-motorcycle-exhausts-among-children-new-south-wales-continuing-problem)
- Single-case report (2022): a 42-year-old man's foot, leg and back of thigh were pinned against a hot exhaust pipe after a motorbike collision. The deep contact burn reached muscle, tendon and bone (fourth-degree-type injury) and needed a free flap; he walked independently at discharge. — [MATSUURA 2022](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/doaj_06ab967c93a64f65b7601f2b0b45f9af/Description)

### Inferences
- Motorcycles are ubiquitous in Indonesia, and children often ride as passengers, so knalpot burns to the lower leg and calf are probably a common household injury. Given the Australian finding that every admitted child needed grafting, a guidebook should warn that a knalpot burn that is small and round but white, grey or brown, or painless, may be deep. It should be cooled for 20 minutes and checked by a health worker, not just treated at home with toothpaste or ointment. This is an inference from contact-burn physics and the MJA series; I found no Indonesian incidence data.
- Hot-water scalds (bathing water, cooking, hot drinks) are the most important cause in Indonesian children (52% in the RSCM data), so scald appearance and first aid deserve the most space in the guidebook.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed Indonesian or Southeast Asian study on motorcycle exhaust burn depth, healing time or grafting rates was found. Indonesian-language searches ("luka bakar knalpot") returned only student theses and engineering papers on exhaust design.
- A PubMed record on exhaust burns (PMID 15774297) and an *Int J Low Extrem Wounds* 2013 paper (doi:10.1177/1534734613502040) appeared in searches but could not be opened (captcha or blocked), so their findings are not reported.
- Hot cooking-oil burns: no Indonesian or SEA data found in this session.
- No guidance from PERAPI (Perhimpunan Dokter Spesialis Bedah Plastik Rekonstruksi dan Estetik Indonesia) or Indonesian burn units, beyond the PNPK, was retrieved.

## 6. Assessing burns on darker (brown) skin tones

### Takeaway
Evidence specific to burns on brown or dark skin is thin. A 2025 scoping review (via trade press) reports that erythema can look darker, purplish or bluish rather than red on darker skin. Standard scar and colour tools perform inconsistently in Fitzpatrick types IV–VI. Darker skin carries a higher risk of post-burn hypopigmentation, hypertrophic scars and keloids. Standard depth tables (and the PNPK colour column) describe colours as they appear on lighter skin.

### Cited Findings
- Erythema "may appear as darker or purplish-blue discoloration rather than redness in darker skin." Tools such as the Vancouver Scar Scale and DermaLab Combo may give inconsistent results in Fitzpatrick IV–VI. Misjudging the injury could delay treatment. — [DT 2025](https://dermatologytimes.com/view/burn-care-disparities-in-skin-of-color), summarising Nguyen et al., *Wound Repair Regen* 2025
- Post-burn hypopigmentation (melanocyte damage producing a sharp contrast with surrounding skin) is a significant concern in darker skin, and treatments for it are under-researched. Hypertrophic scars and keloids "disproportionately affect" people with darker skin and may be more severe. — [DT 2025](https://dermatologytimes.com/view/burn-care-disparities-in-skin-of-color)
- AAFP lists hyperpigmentation as a long-term complication of partial-thickness burns and advises sun protection after healing. — [AAFP 2020](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2020/0415/p463.html)
- The PNPK depth table's colour words ("merah", "merah muda pucat", "merah muda gelap", "merah tua bercak", "putih") come from ANZBA and carry no note on skin tone. — [PNPK](https://keslan.kemkes.go.id/unduhan/fileunduhan_1610415947_843237.pdf)

### Inferences
- For Indonesian readers (mostly Fitzpatrick III–V), the guidebook should lean on signs that do not depend on colour:
  - blisters
  - moist/wet vs dry/leathery surface
  - blanching: press briefly and see whether the colour returns quickly or not at all (the source tables tie this to depth)
  - pain: present vs absent
  - healing time
- Suggested colour wording for brown skin (adapted, not directly sourced):
  - a superficial burn may look "darker than surrounding skin, reddish-brown or purplish" rather than bright red
  - a deeper burn may look "paler or greyish-white, or dark brown/black and leathery"
- Readers should be told to expect colour differences, lighter or darker patches, after healing; the PNPK's "perbedaan warna" note supports this. They should also be told that thick or raised scars (keloid) are more likely on brown skin, especially when healing takes more than 2–3 weeks. Sun protection of healed burns is advisable.

### Gaps
- The darker-skin findings come only from a trade-press summary of a 2025 scoping review; I could not access the primary review. No burn-specific clinical guideline (ABA, ISBI, ANZBA, PNPK) giving depth-colour descriptors for brown or black skin was found.
- Wounds International / Wounds UK skin-tone consensus documents (2023) appeared in searches but were not opened. They concern general wound and pressure-injury assessment, not burns specifically.
- No Indonesian or Southeast Asian data on post-burn scarring or pigmentation outcomes by skin type were found.
